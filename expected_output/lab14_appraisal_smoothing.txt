====================================================================================
WHAT THIS FILE DOES NOT DO
====================================================================================
It does not demonstrate the illiquid-asset case.  Smoothing the S&P leaves
SPX options written on it, and Section 7 says the presence of an options
market is what decides between the two substitutes.  A synthetic illiquid
asset that keeps a liquid asset's options market proves nothing about the
case the paper cares about, and this file does not pretend otherwise.

What it tests is narrower and answerable: whether the CLEAN-LAG model of
staleness behaves like the BLUR that appraisal-based valuation produces.

benchmark, fully current and sharp: R2 = 0.4987

====================================================================================
READING THE FIRST TWO COLUMNS
====================================================================================
Blur minus lag, across the grid: -96% to -72%.
The two designs are nothing alike, and the reason is simple enough that it
should have been anticipated: an exponentially weighted average STILL
CONTAINS TODAY'S OBSERVATION, with weight alpha.  Blur is therefore a much
milder handicap than lag - own R2 falls only 0.499 to 0.456 across a mean
lag of 55 days, against 0.499 to -0.035 under a clean lag - and with so
little damage to repair, the substitution rate has almost no denominator
and is not interpretable.  Blur alone is the WRONG model of a stale mark.

====================================================================================
THE DESIGN THAT ACTUALLY MATCHES A QUARTERLY MARK
====================================================================================
A marked-to-appraisal holder sees a number REVISED every q days and HELD
flat in between, revised by partial adjustment rather than fully.  That is
staircase staleness plus blur, and it is the combination the paper's
motivation actually describes.

VERDICT
====================================================================================
  Judged on the GAP between point estimates, not on interval overlap:
  at short lags the clean-lag interval is so wide that anything falls
  inside it, which would make 'overlap' a meaningless test.

  q= 5 alpha=1.00  mean lag   2.0 -> R =  24%;  clean lag delta= 1 -> R =  -1%;  gap  +25%  DIFFERS
  q=21 alpha=1.00  mean lag  10.0 -> R =  64%;  clean lag delta= 8 -> R =  60%;  gap   +4%  match
  q=63 alpha=1.00  mean lag  31.0 -> R =  71%;  clean lag delta=34 -> R =  70%;  gap   +1%  match
  q=21 alpha=0.50  mean lag  31.0 -> R =  63%;  clean lag delta=34 -> R =  70%;  gap   -7%  match
  q=63 alpha=0.50  mean lag  94.0 -> R =  70%;  clean lag delta=55 -> R =  72%;  gap   -1%  match

  The q = 5 row is the exception and is not evidence of anything: at a
  mean lag of two days the clean lag has destroyed almost nothing, so the
  substitution rate has a denominator near zero and is unstable in both
  designs.  The four rows with real staleness agree to within eight points.

Two things follow, and the first is a correction to this file's own
starting assumption.  Blur ALONE is not a model of a stale mark: an
exponentially weighted average still contains today's observation, so it
costs almost nothing and leaves no damage for breadth to repair.  What a
quarterly-marked holder has is STAIRCASE staleness - a number revised on a
schedule and held flat between revisions - and that design DOES track the
clean lag closely at matched mean staleness.  A clean lag is therefore a
usable stand-in for periodic marking, which is what Section 11 needs.

The partial-adjustment rows sit a few points below what mean-lag matching
predicts, so the equivalence is approximate and the paper should not claim
more than approximate.  And none of this is evidence about property: the
asset is still the S&P, it still has options written on it, and Section 7's
verdict on implied volatility is untouched by everything above.
