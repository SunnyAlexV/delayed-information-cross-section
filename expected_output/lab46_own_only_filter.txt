1.  THE PROJECTION, IMPLEMENTED EXACTLY AS ASKED
================================================================================================
Hand the ridge phi^(delta+h) xhat(t-delta) instead of xhat(t-delta).
Same feature, same days, same refit schedule; one is projected forward
and the other is not.

     3  0.389211    0.389211    -1.59e-13

    13  0.219899    0.219899    -1.71e-12
    21  0.133880    0.133880    -3.99e-12
    55  0.014535    0.014535    -1.73e-11

  largest difference the projection makes: 1.7e-11

  It makes none, and it cannot. At a fixed delay the projection is one
  constant per column; the ridge standardises each column before
  fitting; and (c x - c mu) / (c sd) = (x - mu) / sd. The coefficient
  simply absorbs the constant. What residue there is comes from the
  1e-9 added to the standard deviation to keep it from vanishing.

  So the objection cannot bite through the projection. It can only bite
  through the state itself - if a filtered history carries something the
  paper's three features do not already span. Part 2 tests that.

2.  THE SMOOTHING, WHICH IS THE PART THAT COULD MATTER
================================================================================================
The paper's control is the stale level and two rectangular means, 5 and
22 days. A filter weights the whole history exponentially instead. So
give the control both: the three features it has, plus exponentially
weighted averages at half-lives (5, 22, 66) days. Six features against
three, nested, so anything the filter knows is in there somewhere.

  Three extra columns, every one of them a weighted average of a
  history the model already has, and the control gets worse - by more
  the staler it is. Nothing was added to the information set; what was
  added was six coefficients to estimate where there had been three,
  on a window that does not grow. This is Section 7's cost of breadth
  arriving inside the domestic block, and it is the reason the paper's
  control is three features rather than every feature available.

  The middle column is the referees' mechanism caught in the act. The
  exponential control is the weaker one, and R measured against it is
  higher at every delay, by up to 4.1% - 74.3% at eleven weeks
  against the published 71.4%. A weak control inflates the rate, exactly
  as the algebra says; the question was only ever which control is weak.
  Here it is the alternative, so the published figure is the smaller of
  the two on offer and the objection has nothing left to correct.

  Which way a move runs is not obvious from the control alone. R reads
  the own-only curve at two points, and the origin sets the denominator,
  so a control that improves mostly at delta = 0 pushes the rate DOWN
  twice over, while one that improves only at long delays can push it up.

================================================================================================
4.  DOES A BETTER RULER TIGHTEN THE EFFECTIVE-AGE INTERVAL?
================================================================================================
A referee predicted it would, 'dramatically'. The interval is a block
bootstrap over TEST DAYS, so the prediction is testable: rebuild the
ruler from the stronger own-only curve, resample the same way, and
compare the widths. Width in days is not the comparison - a ruler that
decays faster reads every skill as a younger age and shrinks the
interval whether or not the measurement got sharper - so the width is
also reported as a share of the point it surrounds.

================================================================================================
VERDICT
================================================================================================
  The projection the objection names changes nothing at all (2e-11),
  and the reason is algebraic rather than empirical: a standardised
  linear model absorbs any deterministic rescaling of its inputs. A
  filter can only help through the state it builds, so the test that
  matters is the exponential one.

  Exponential weighting of the whole history beats the paper's two
  rectangular windows at 0 of 6 delays, and loses by more the staler
  the control is. The own-only control is not the straw man the
  objection assumes: it is the strongest arm of the three tried here,
  and the rate at eleven weeks stays at 71.4%. Measured against the
  weaker exponential control it would read 74.3%, which is the
  inflation the referees warned about, pointing the other way.

  The effective-age interval moves by -7% as a share of the point it
  surrounds, which is not the dramatic tightening predicted. Nor could it
  be: that width is sampling uncertainty over test days, and changing the
  ruler changes the age a forecast reads as, not how much that reading
  varies from one resampled window to the next. Section 9.3 keeps the
  paper's ruler, which reads the headline as the older and therefore less
  flattering 4.6 days rather than 4.2. The prediction was the right kind of
  claim and the wrong answer, and it was cheaper to test than to argue
  about.
