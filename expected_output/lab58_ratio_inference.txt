========================================================================================
0.  THE BENCHMARK CANCELS, SO R IS A RATIO OF TWO MEAN DIFFERENCES
========================================================================================
R = [S_alt - S_own] / [S_own(0) - S_own(d)] with a COMMON benchmark on
the same days.  Claimed identity: R = mean(L_own,d - L_alt,d) / mean(
L_own,d - L_own,0).  Checked on random losses rather than argued:

  from skills      1.558101087292
  from differences 1.558101087292
  identical -> True

  The benchmark contributes NO sampling error to R, and the weak-
  denominator problem is exactly mean(d2) near zero, where d2 is the
  per-day damage the delay does.

========================================================================================
1.  WHICH R IS THE INTERVAL SUPPOSED TO COVER?
========================================================================================
A referee asked what the inferential object is, and the question is not
rhetorical: this design has two candidates and they are not close together.

  CONDITIONAL   the rate this forecaster would achieve on THIS history, in
                expectation over the noise.  It is what Giacomini-White
                inference targets and what the paper's tests are built on.
  POPULATION    the rate the DGP implies, averaging over histories too.

The global factor is persistent, so a single history is a small effective
sample and the two objects separate.  Measured below rather than asserted.

  R on one fixed history, three noise draws: 0.462, 0.502, 0.523
  R on three different histories:            0.402, 0.552, 0.469

  Holding the history fixed, R barely moves.  Changing the history moves
  it a great deal.  An interval built by resampling DAYS can only speak
  to the first, so the conditional rate is the object and Part 2 tests
  coverage of it.  Part 3 asks what that leaves unsaid.

========================================================================================
3.  WHAT NO DAY-RESAMPLING INTERVAL CAN DO
========================================================================================
Every procedure here resamples days within one history, so none of them
prices the uncertainty that comes from WHICH history was drawn.  With a
persistent common factor that second source is not small, and it is invisible
to all four methods equally.  The spread below is across histories.
     strong  sd of R across histories  0.148   implied 95% spread  0.582   median interval width  0.383
   moderate  sd of R across histories  0.133   implied 95% spread  0.521   median interval width  0.530
       weak  sd of R across histories  0.291   implied 95% spread  1.142   median interval width  1.306

  This is a statement about what the interval MEANS, not a defect in
  it: a conditional interval is a claim about the history in hand, and
  the paper has one history. It is the reason the paper reports a span
  across specifications rather than leaning on a single interval.

========================================================================================
4.  VERDICT
========================================================================================
  iid bootstrap                 64%, 52%, 75%
  block boot 2h  (the paper's)  92%, 86%, 94%
  block boot 6h                 96%, 91%, 98%
  block boot 12h                98%, 92%, 96%
  delta/HAC 2h                  94%, 89%, 99%
  delta/HAC 12h                 98%, 91%, 98%
  Fieller/HAC 2h                95%, 89%, 98%
  Fieller/HAC 12h               98%, 92%, 98%

  The paper's block length is too short. The loss differences inherit
  the persistence of the common factor as well as the h-day overlap,
  so a block of 2h leaves dependence inside the resample and the
  interval comes out too narrow. Lengthening the block fixes it, and
  that is a correction the paper should adopt rather than defend.
  Fieller at a long lag is the best-covering procedure tested, which
  is the argument for quoting it rather than merely checking with it.

========================================================================================
5.  HOW LONG THE BLOCK HAS TO BE, MEASURED ON THE REAL LOSSES
========================================================================================
The simulation says a short block under-covers, but it cannot say what
length THIS data needs: that depends on the dependence in the real loss
differences, not on the persistence chosen for a generator. So it is measured.
Below is the autocorrelation of d1 and d2 at two delays, computed from the
paper's own forecasts, and the lag at which it first falls below 0.05.

 delta  series   lag1   lag5  lag10  lag20  lag30  lag40  lag60  <0.05 at
     8      d1   0.89   0.34   0.12   0.01  -0.03  -0.05   0.01        13
     8      d2   0.89   0.35   0.13   0.03  -0.00  -0.05  -0.01        14
    55      d1   0.94   0.67   0.48   0.22   0.11   0.02  -0.02        38
    55      d2   0.95   0.66   0.48   0.24   0.12   0.02  -0.03        37

  At the paper's headline delay the loss differences still carry
  0.48 autocorrelation at lag 10, which is the block length an
  earlier version of this paper used. Dependence does not die out until
  lag 38. The block is therefore set to 40 days, which outlasts it at
  every delay, and 121 blocks remain in a sample of 4862.

============================================================================================
6.  THE HAC BANDWIDTH, MEASURED RATHER THAN TAKEN FROM A RULE
============================================================================================
Part 5 set the BLOCK from the measured dependence. Every HAC standard
error in this project answers the same question and, until this was
measured, answered it with the automatic rule 4 (n/100)^(2/9). That is
9 lags here, and part 5 has just shown the differential still carries
half its autocorrelation there. A kernel truncated inside the
dependence understates the standard error, which inflates every
statistic built from it - always in the direction that flatters a
result.

Three estimators are compared. Two are HAC at different bandwidths and
one is a moving-block bootstrap, which makes no kernel assumption at
all. If the bandwidth is long enough they agree; where they disagree,
the short one is wrong.

 delta      HAC(9)     HAC(40)     HAC(80)        iid    boot SD(40)  short/long
     0    0.004221    0.004495    0.004295   0.002110       0.004615        0.94
     5    0.016868    0.019470    0.019021   0.007579       0.019924        0.87
    21    0.041852    0.058185    0.059100   0.015218       0.057837        0.72
    55    0.049456    0.075210    0.080987   0.017548       0.076382        0.66

  The automatic bandwidth understates the standard error by up to
  34% at the delays above, and it is the longest delays - the ones
  the paper leads with - where it is worst. Doubling the bandwidth from
  40 to 80 moves it little and the bootstrap, which assumes no kernel,
  lands beside them both, so 40 is long enough and the choice is not
  delicate. HAC_LAG is therefore set to the block length: both answer
  'how far does the dependence run', and letting them answer it
  differently is what left a simultaneous band studentised by one and
  resampled by the other.
