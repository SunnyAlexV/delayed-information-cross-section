S1. Robustness of the measurement

R(δ) carries Sown in three of its four places, so every figure above is a statement about the domestic model as much as about the foreign one. That is uncomfortable, because the paper has spent its effort on the other side: Section 6.1 compresses the foreign block, Section S27 splits it, Section S28 tries it as one regressor. The control has been the same three features throughout. This section varies it, and then varies three other things that could make the headline wrong: the sample period, the number of models we tried before arriving at this one, and the gap between training and forecast. Each was run once and is reported whatever it said.

S2. Statistical inference · Result survives

The bootstrap intervals of Section 5 quantify sampling uncertainty in a ratio of realised out-of-sample losses, and an earlier version of this section dismissed them as unable to test anything at all. That was too strong: any interval can be inverted into a test of the values it excludes, and a reader is right to object to a claim that says otherwise. What is true is narrower, and it is what the rest of this section rests on. Those intervals are built for R(δ) rather than for the loss differential, and they carry no adjustment for the fact that our domestic model is nested inside our cross-sectional one, which is exactly the case in which the usual Diebold-Mariano comparison loses its standard distribution. Diebold has written at length about the misuse of his own test in exactly this situation (2015). So the equal-predictive-ability question is given a test of its own rather than read off a rate interval that happens to exclude zero. We use Giacomini-White (2006) as that test, and as the primary test of this paper, because it is valid for nested models and, being loss-agnostic, covers the QLIKE result as well as the squared-error one. It requires a fixed rolling estimation window, of the kind Section 4 describes. Writing Ltown and Ltalt for the two models' losses on test day t and Dt = Ltown − Ltalt for the loss differential, the statistic is

GW = d ÷ SE(d),     SE(d)² = (1/n) [ γ0 + 2 ∑j=1L (1 − j/(L+1)) γj ],(14)

where d is the mean of Dt over the n test days, γj is its sample autocovariance at lag j, and the Bartlett weights (1 − j/(L+1)) run to L = 40 lags. Both parts of that sentence need defending. The HAC correction is not optional: a five-day-ahead target overlaps on four of every five days, so the raw standard error of a daily loss differential understates by roughly a factor of three. And the bandwidth is measured rather than taken from the usual automatic rule, which gives 9 lags at this sample size and truncates the kernel while the differential still carries 0.52 autocorrelation. Section S13 reports the measurement: at eleven weeks the automatic bandwidth returns 0.0495 against 0.0752 at forty lags and 0.0810 at eighty, while a moving-block bootstrap, which assumes no kernel at all, returns 0.0760. The three agree once the bandwidth outlasts the dependence and the short one does not, so L is set to the block length of Section 5: both answer the same question and letting them answer it differently is what left an earlier simultaneous band studentised by one and resampled by the other. Every statistic this corrects was inflated, which is to say every correction here is against the paper's own result. Positive GW favours the model with the substitute, and we report two-sided p-values from the standard normal throughout. Clark-West (2007) serves as a second opinion on squared loss, with a qualification developed in Section S3 that is the reason it is the second opinion, not the first.

S3. A caveat on the Clark-West column · Claim narrowed

Clark-West is the second opinion on squared loss and carries a qualification worth stating. Its correction term assumes the larger model nests the smaller exactly; a ridge with a cross-validated penalty satisfies that only approximately, because shrinkage pulls the larger model away from the nesting, and the correction can then flatter it. The diagnostic below measures how far from exact the nesting is at each delay, and the remedy imposes a single ridge penalty on both arms, selected on the restricted one. That is the fix the objection calls for rather than a different one: the correction is flattered when the two arms are shrunk by different amounts, so equalising the shrinkage closes that channel, and it does so without leaving the estimator the rest of the paper uses. It should be said exactly what the remedy buys. A common penalty reduces the departure from exact nesting rather than completely removing it. A ridge fit on the wider design is not the ridge fit on the narrower one with the extra coefficients set to zero, whatever penalty the two are given, because the penalty acts on a different number of standardised columns in each; some departure therefore survives any choice of common penalty. What the remedy removes is the differential-shrinkage part, which is the part that moves with δ and the part the diagnostic below measures. The residual part is common to both arms and small by construction, but it is not zero, and we do not claim it is. An unpenalised refit would also make the nesting exact and would answer a different question, about a model this paper never runs. The diagnostic is a ratio with no established critical value. We offer that ratio as a description, not a threshold, and lean on the common-penalty re-run instead of on where the ratio happens to fall.

The primary test is untouched, and for a reason, not by luck. The argument above turns on Clark-West being a test about models, so that anything changing the estimation penalty changes what its adjustment ought to be. Giacomini-White is a test about methods under a fixed rolling window, and a method is the whole procedure including whatever tuning happens inside that window. The validation slice is carved out of the training window, so it is part of what is being tested rather than a contaminant of a correction, and Giacomini-White makes no finite-sample correction that could be thrown off. The prediction is that its column barely moves under the remedy, and the last two columns of Table S2 are that prediction tested: no statistic moves by more than 0.06 and no verdict changes at any delay. The substitution rates, the bootstrap intervals and the simultaneous bands involve no nested-model correction at all and are unaffected.

A second concern is that we test ten delays at once. Ten pointwise 95% intervals will breach somewhere by chance, so we bootstrapped the whole vector jointly, in moving blocks of forty days to respect the overlap in the target (Künsch 1989), and took the largest studentised deviation across the grid. The resulting critical value is 2.42 instead of 1.96, a heavy penalty, and the effect still clears it from δ = 3 to δ = 55. One casualty is worth naming: the small negative reading at δ = 0 does not survive the simultaneous band. We do not claim the cross-section is harmful when domestic data is current, and Section 6 gives the reason it is not: net of what it costs to estimate, its content there is zero. That casualty is worth using to fix a vocabulary, because three different statements get confused in this literature and this paper needs all three. Pointwise: an individual interval excludes zero, which is what the cells of Table 2 report. Simultaneous: the effect survives a band covering the whole delay grid at once, which is the 2.42 critical value above and which δ = 0 fails. Equivalence: the effect lies inside a margin declared negligible in advance, which this paper tests nowhere and therefore never claims. A cell that fails the first is one where no incremental contribution was detected; it is not a cell where the contribution is zero, and wherever an interval still admits a contribution worth having we say what that interval permits rather than rounding it to nothing.

The third concern is that the result is an artefact of fitting a wide model to a persistent series. We tested it by shifting the entire foreign block five years through the sample. The series keep their distributions, their autocorrelation, their crises; they lose only their alignment with the target. The substitution rate falls from +72% to −8%, and is negative at every delay we checked. Whatever the model is using, it is the alignment. The residual negative is not noise around zero and should not be read as one: it is the cost of breadth from Section 6, which is all that remains once the information is removed. A placebo of this kind lands below zero by construction, and a version of it that came back at exactly zero would be the surprising result.

Finally, Section 5 described the rate as slowing, which is a claim, not an observation. Comparing R(55) with earlier values: against R(13) the difference is +0.079 with an interval of [+0.037, +0.129], and against R(21) it is +0.042 with [+0.014, +0.076], both still rising. Against R(34) it is +0.018 with [−0.001, +0.039], which does not reject equality. The rate is still climbing out to about seven weeks and flat thereafter.

One last property of the rate was assumed and never tested. The rate is a ratio whose denominator is what the delay costs a domestic-only forecaster, and at short delays that quantity is near zero. A ratio with a denominator that can vanish is weakly identified, and a percentile bootstrap of it reports finite endpoints whether or not they mean anything. We therefore recomputed every interval by inverting a HAC t-test after Fieller, which returns two half-lines or the whole line when the data do not pin the ratio down. At every delay from one day to eleven weeks the confidence set is an ordinary interval, and Fieller's endpoints sit within 1.6 percentage points of the bootstrap's throughout: at δ = 55, [59%, 81%] against [60%, 81%]. The objection is right in principle and inert here, so the published intervals stand. Section 4.6 of the paper gives the construction, the one delay where the ratio does not exist at all, and the error that revealed the first version to be wrong.

S4. The domestic control and the level normalisation · Magnitude falls

Five stronger domestic blocks were built, each a superset of the paper's: four further own lags; quarterly and annual means; leverage terms from the signed return; a second realised measure together with the LEVEL of the trailing median; and all of them at once. Each was given the same walk-forward, the same refit schedule and the same ridge, and R(δ) was recomputed inside each arm, so every rate is measured against its own control. The strongest was chosen on own-only skill averaged across delays, never on the substitution rate, the quantity under test and must not be allowed to select its own control.

The control was improvable and the rate moves. A richer domestic block beats the paper's in 11 of 45 cells, and the substitution rate falls by three to four points at every delay, significantly at δ = 34 and 55. Two things about that last clause. The direction is the same at all eight delays and the size barely varies, so what separates the significant delays from the rest is power rather than mechanism, and we read the column as one result rather than as two. And the list was four delays long under the nine-lag HAC kernel, which Section S13 shows truncates inside the dependence; at the measured bandwidth the table above carries, δ = 13 and 21 fall just short, at [−9.9, +0.5] and [−9.1, +0.3]. Four points is not a reversal, since 68% sits inside the [60, 81] interval Table 2 already carries, but the paired test is sharper than the overlap and it excludes zero at the two longest delays, so the gap is real and the paper quotes both figures and leans on neither.

What makes that arm strong is the part worth keeping. It carries two features and they are not the same kind of thing. One is a second realised measure, a 22-day Yang-Zhang, which is simply less noisy than the 5-day one; the other is log Mt, the LEVEL of the trailing median. Splitting them settles it at once. At eleven weeks the paper's block scores −0.0351, adding the second measure scores -0.0428, which is worse, and adding the level alone scores +0.0308 The level does the work, at nine delays out of nine, and by a margin that grows with staleness: +0.0011 of R² at zero delay against +0.0736 at eleven weeks.

That is a finding about this paper's own design, not about volatility. Equation (2) divides every series by its own trailing median, and that is what puts Tokyo, São Paulo and the VIX on one scale and makes a cross-section possible at all. It is also what prevents every model in the paper from seeing how volatile the world actually is. A forecaster who has lost his recent observations still knows roughly what regime he was in, and that knowledge is worth more the more he has lost, which is where the paper claims its largest effect. The comparability the design buys is paid for in domestic information, the price rises with delay, and we have not found this trade priced anywhere in the literature the paper cites. Anyone normalising a panel this way should measure what it costs before assuming it is free.

That claim has an obvious objection and it has to be answered rather than noted. The target is log(σ² / Mt+5), M is a 252-day median and therefore very slow, so a model handed log Mt−δ could lower its squared error simply by predicting the target's own DENOMINATOR. That would be arithmetic about how the target was built, not a forecast of volatility. Nothing would be inadmissible, since every feature is dated t − δ, but the finding would be empty.

The test is to re-date the normaliser and see what survives. Three targets were built, differing in nothing else: the paper's, normalised at t+5; the origin-dated one of Section 4, normalised at t; and one normalised at t − δ, the forecaster's own information set. The third is decisive, because its denominator is known exactly when the forecast is made, so a model cannot buy anything by predicting a quantity it has already been given, and it is also the only one of the three a delayed forecaster could actually use. Across the four positive delays the third target retains between 87% and 103% of the gain: +0.0656 against +0.0638 at eleven weeks. Re-dating the normaliser changes nothing, so the level is not being used to undo the normalisation. It is information.

Two qualifications. The gain reaches conventional significance only at δ = 55, on all three targets alike, so the short-delay column shows a direction without establishing a result. And the rate correction above survives the same test: on the implementable target the level moves R(55) from 73% to 69%, a shift of the same size as the 72% to 68% reported here.

A third objection reaches past the feature list to the estimator class. Every arm above hands a delayed observation to a ridge as a stale number, where a state-space model would project it forward, so the comparison could be said to test feature counts and never the class of estimator. The obvious such model was fitted on the domestic series alone: a first-order state observed with noise, with γ(0) = q/(1 − φ²) + r and γ(k) = φkq/(1 − φ²), so that φ = γ(2)/γ(1) and the three parameters come from the first three autocovariances of the first 1,500 rows. They are then frozen, and no foreign series enters anywhere. The moments give φ = 0.9265, a half-life of 9.1 trading days, and an observation-noise variance of zero to five decimal places. That last figure is already part of the answer: with no measurement noise the Kalman gain is one and the filtered state is the observation, to 4.4 × 10−8, so at daily frequency there is nothing for the update step to clean up.

The projection is the rest of it, and it is algebra rather than evidence. A forecaster δ days late projects by φδ+h, which multiplies a column by one constant; the ridge standardises each column before fitting; and (cx − cμ)/(cσ) = (x − μ)/σ. The coefficient absorbs the constant and the forecast does not move. Run both arms on the same days and the largest difference across six delays is 1.8 × 10−11, which is the 10−9 guard added to the standard deviation and nothing else. At a fixed delay, projecting a state forward cannot improve any scale-equivariant linear forecaster, whatever else it does for the forecaster's intuition. The objection has to enter through the state itself, if a filtered history spans something the paper's three features do not.

It does not. The paper's control is the stale level and rectangular means over 5 and 22 days; a filter weights the whole history exponentially, so the control was given both, with exponentially weighted averages at half-lives of 5, 22 and 66 days added to what it had. Six features against three, and the six-feature block is worse at every delay tested: by 0.0018 of R² at the origin, 0.0321 at δ = 21 and 0.0522 at eleven weeks, losing by more the staler it is. Nothing was added to the information set, since every new column is a weighted average of a history the model already held. What was added was six coefficients to estimate on a training window that does not grow, which is Section S2's cost of breadth arriving inside the domestic block.

The rate then moves in the direction the objection did not anticipate, and the reason is worth stating once in general. Write the substitution rate as N/D with N the error the cross-section removes and D the error the delay adds. Raising Sown(δ) by e subtracts e from both, and (N − e)/(D − e) < N/D whenever N < D, which holds here at every delay. A weak control inflates R, exactly as the objection says. The question was only ever which control is the weak one. Measured against the exponential block, R(δ) is higher at all five delays, by up to 4.1 points, reading 74.3% at eleven weeks where the paper's control reads 71.4% and Table S8 rounds it to 71%. The alternative is the arm that inflates. What survives this section is the correction in Table S3, which comes from the level of the trailing median and not from the class of estimator. Section S12's ruler behaves the same way: rebuilt from the exponential curve it reads the headline as 4.2 days rather than 4.6, and its interval is 7% narrower as a share of the point it surrounds, so the paper keeps the reading that flatters it less.

Three attacks on the control have now been answered one at a time, and answering an objection three times is not the same as retiring it, because a fourth control can always be named. So the control is priced as a set instead. Eight domestic blocks were run through the same walk-forward: the paper's, the four supersets of Table S3, all of them at once, the exponential block above and the projected state above. At eleven weeks the substitution rate across the eight runs from 60.8% to 74.4%. The strongest by own-only skill averaged across delays is the arm carrying the level, at 0.3022 against the paper's 0.2803, and the rate inside it is 67.5%, a paired difference of −4.1% [−9.7, −0.7] from the paper's 71.6%. That reproduces Table S3's four points from a second direction, on a different feature set and a different script, which is worth more than a number agreeing with itself. One bookkeeping point, because this section now quotes the paper's own control twice and the two figures differ. The state-space comparison above reads it as 71.4% and the envelope reads it as 71.6%, and Table 2 reads it as 72%. Each file builds its panel on the delay grid its own comparison needs, so each starts its test window a few days from the others. The spread is the third significant figure of a quantity whose interval is [60, 81], and every comparison drawn from it is internal to the file that made it.

One rule has to be stated because the obvious choice is wrong. The lowest figure in that set is 60.8%, and it is not the conservative rate. The arm producing it scores 0.5748 own-only at the origin and −0.0648 at eleven weeks, so it is the strongest control in the set where the denominator is set and among the weakest where the numerator is. R(δ) reads the own-only curve at two points, and an arm strong at one and weak at the other moves the rate without being a better control anywhere. Quoting it would be the mirror of the error this section is answering, made in the paper's own favour, so selection stays on own-only skill and is fixed before the rates are read.

What the envelope retires is the open-ended form of the objection. The control is no longer one choice a reader takes on trust: eight are priced, the rule is stated in advance, and a ninth moves the headline only by beating all eight on own-only skill. One further check belongs here rather than in Section 7, because it is about the control rather than about the candidates. Section 7's redundancy result was established with the paper's domestic block underneath both, and a stronger block could in principle leave less room for the options market too. Re-run inside the level-carrying control on Section 7's own window, the foreign block adds nothing detectable at any of six delays, with statistics from −1.94 to −2.86, every one of them more negative than with the paper's control. Section 7 does not rest on a weak domestic model either.

S5. Temporal stability · Result survives

Section S10 conditions on market state; this asks the different question of whether the result drifts in time. Split at the midpoint, the two halves agree at every delay, 77% against 67% at eleven weeks, with overlapping intervals at all four. A rolling window tells a less settled story: R(5) runs from 22% in the window ending April 2019 to 91% in the window ending April 2015, and a line through those points falls about 22 points a decade at five days and 12 at eleven weeks. Those windows overlap by 750 of 1,000 days, so the path is descriptive and no standard error is quoted for it.

The obvious explanation is composition, since the first half holds 2008, 2011 and 2015 and Section S10 has already shown the rate is higher in stress. Holding stress fixed settles it: comparing the halves within a VIX tercile, not one of twelve cells differs significantly. The drift in the path cannot be attributed to a change in how the cross-section works, and on this sample it cannot be attributed to anything.

Conditioning does something else, though, and it is the reason Section 5 quotes the rate by state.

So the conditional reading stands, and on absolute error as well as on the ratio. The calm column is not a small number, it is an imprecise one: it spans most of the unit interval and at three of the four delays it does not exclude zero. We suspected worse. A ratio whose denominator can approach zero need not have a bounded confidence set at all, and a percentile bootstrap cannot express that, so the Fieller construction of Section 4.6 of the paper was applied to all twelve cells. Every one comes back an ordinary interval, and within a point or two of the bootstrap's: the calm cell at five days is [−4, 104] against the bootstrap's [−4, 102], and the stressed cell at eleven weeks is [74, 87] against the bootstrap's [74, 87], which agree to the digit. The denominator never approaches zero closely enough to misbehave, so the printed intervals stand and the calm rate is imprecise without being unidentified. That is not a failure of the estimator but a property of the question: where delay costs a forecaster almost nothing, the share of that cost which the cross-section repairs has no stable value. The calm-market rate is weakly measured and economically uninformative in this sample, but the Fieller analysis does not classify it as unidentified: every calm-state confidence set it returns is bounded, if very wide. An unconditional rate therefore averages a well-measured result with a poorly measured one, and Section 5 says so instead of quoting the average alone.

One more thing can be asked of the calm cell before it is set aside, and it should be. The paper has chosen a coordinate that fails in a third of its sample and reported the failure as a fact about markets, and a reader is entitled to suspect the coordinate instead. The effective age of Section S12 is the alternative and it is built differently: it asks how old a plain mark would have to be to forecast as badly, so it has no denominator that can vanish, and taken in mean squared error rather than in skill it carries no benchmark variance either, which is the other quantity that goes small in a calm market.

Asked that way the calm cell still does not answer. The age is 4.5 days with an interval of [0.3, 44.1], which is 7.8 times as wide relative to its own point as the stressed cell's, and 47 of 1,500 replications cannot place it on the grid at all. The non-result therefore survives the change of instrument, and that is worth more to a reader than a number would have been: where delay costs a forecaster almost nothing there is almost nothing for the cross-section to repair, and no coordinate makes a share of nothing precise. The limitation belongs to the question and not to the ratio.

The same construction pays at the other end of the ladder, where the quantity is well determined and the unit is the one a practitioner uses. In the top tercile of VIX days a fifty-five-day-old mark plus today's foreign closes forecasts like a mark 3.8 days old [1.9, 6.6]; in the top decile, 3.4 [1.3, 7.2]; in the top 5% of days, 2.3 [0.8, 5.0]. The ladder is monotone in days as well as in shares. On the days when a stale mark is most dangerous, eleven weeks of staleness plus a current cross-section is worth a mark two days old.

Conditioning on the VIX raises a fair objection, since Section 7 says that a forecaster who holds the VIX should ignore the cross-section altogether. The two are compatible, but only for a specific reader, and it is worth being exact about who. The VIX is a public index on the S&P; a holder of an illiquid asset can see it whether or not any option trades on the thing they own. Such a reader can therefore condition on market state without being able to buy the substitute that state variable makes redundant. A reader who holds options on their own asset is in Section 7's case and should stop reading at Section 8. We also note that Section S10 works with a ladder of cuts at the 33rd, 90th and 95th percentiles while this section uses terciles, and that the tercile split was chosen after that ladder had been seen.

S6. Multiple-testing adjustment · Result survives

Forty-nine scripts stand behind this paper and nine distinct forecasters have been fitted to this target across them. A single test statistic on the one that won is not the right instrument for that, so the whole set is put through White's Reality Check and Hansen's SPA, with the size of both first validated by simulation on this sample's own dependence structure, where a nominal 5% test rejects 5.5% and 7.0% of the time respectively, with SPA rejecting more often, as Hansen predicts. Against the domestic benchmark, the best of the nine survives both tests at δ = 3, 5, 13, 21 and 55 with p below 0.0005. It fails at δ = 0, where the best margin across all nine models is +0.0014 of R²: nothing beats the benchmark there at all, so that failure is an absence of any effect to correct.

S7. Leakage from an overlapping target · Result survives

The target is realised variance over the next five days, so consecutive observations share four days of outcome, and a naive split would put part of the answer in the training set. Every refit here already cuts the training window at t − δ − h, which removes exactly the rows whose outcome was unresolved at the forecast date: a purge, though the paper has not called it one. Removing it inflates R² by +0.0024 [+0.00062, +0.00387] at zero delay and +0.0021 [+0.00017, +0.00382] at three days, so it is load-bearing and any study of a five-day target that splits without one is reporting inflated numbers. Adding an embargo on top of it costs nothing detectable in any of eighteen cells, so the gap the design already leaves is sufficient.

S9. Where each result holds

The same exercise with each of five indices as the target is in Table S6, on the continuous target and at every delay.

The effect appears in all five, and the Nikkei is the case that carries information. It closes before every other market in the sample, so it has no same-day foreign observation at all, since every peer reaches it a session late, and it recovers the least, 60% against 69% to 88% elsewhere. That is what the mechanism predicts and what a spurious correlation would not produce. Past that contrast we can say little: the S&P has six same-day peers and recovers less than the FTSE with four, the intervals overlap heavily, and five markets cannot resolve an ordering. Five markets also cannot show where the effect stops, and it does stop: Section S14 adds four targets outside this group on two of which the cross-section is worth less than nothing, and identifies what separates them from these five.

One further objection deserves an answer. Rapach, Strauss and Zhou find that lagged US returns predict foreign returns while the reverse is weak (2013). That concerns returns while ours concerns volatility, and lagged returns where ours are same-day closes observed before the target's own close. We rely on both distinctions, and a reader who rejects them should discount Sections 4 and 5 accordingly.

The scope condition matters more than the ordering. Section 7 shows that where a liquid options market exists on the asset, breadth is redundant, and the S&P 500 is the most heavily optioned index in the world. So the breadth result may be most relevant to assets without such a market, the case Section 1 opens with: a private portfolio marked quarterly, a property book on an appraisal cycle, a fund reporting NAV with a lag. Nobody writes a liquid option on those. What a holder of them can see is the public cross-section, and Sections 4 and 5 say what that is worth as a function of how stale the mark has become.

We would rather state that plainly than dress it up, because it is also the weakest point of the design. We ran the experiment on a liquid asset in order to observe the truth, and the property that makes the truth observable, a deep and continuously traded market, is the same property that brings an options market with it. The result we can measure cleanly and the case we care about are therefore separated by the one feature that decides which substitute wins. Section 11 says what would close that gap.

There remains the δ = 0 row of Table 2, and its history is worth recording because the reason for the caution changed. On log-scale squared error the cross-section is worse by 0.0099, interval [−0.0191, −0.0011]; on QLIKE it is worse by 0.0127, [−0.0263, −0.0010]; on squared error on the natural variance scale it is worse by 0.0037, [−0.0310, +0.0180]. All three point the same way and two of the three exclude zero. That is not what this section said until Section 4.4's reconstruction was corrected. On the median-normalised scale the same comparison comes out at +0.0070, [−0.0429, +0.0204], and this section used to read that as the sign depending on the scale. It does not. The normalised loss weights each day's squared error by that day's trailing median, so it is a weighted loss that had been described as an unweighted one; the unweighted natural-scale loss is the third figure above, and it agrees in sign with the other two. The flip was an artefact of a weighted loss described as an unweighted one, and the claim that a monotone transformation of the target moved the sign was wrong. What remains is smaller but still a reason for caution: the effect at zero delay is about one point of R², and the robust loss that most resembles the headline cannot distinguish it from zero. An earlier draft read the negative as settled and used it to press a disagreement with Korkusuz (2025) and Jayawardena et al. (2020), who find foreign volatility improves a current forecast. We still decline to press it, on the narrower ground that a one-point effect at zero delay on a single target is not the basis for a disagreement, rather than on the ground that we cannot tell its sign. Table 3's decomposition still holds conditional on the log scale: whatever is there is small, bounded near one point of R², and mostly the bill for estimating seven coefficients.

S10. In stress and in calm · Effect larger in stress, unresolvable in calm

Everything above is an average over 4,862 days, and one objection is that the interesting days are not average: a rolling median assumes a stationary volatility distribution, and across a structural macro transition it should fail to adjust. The objection is testable here because the test window contains the 2008 crisis, 2011, August 2015, February 2018, COVID and the 2022 inflation shock. It is not testable on the companion note, whose window begins in November 2023 and contains no transition at all, and the note says so instead of answering it.

Two decisions shape what follows. The first is that a single split is not enough. Six named episodes at full width run to 866 days, most of them ordinary: "the 2022 inflation shock" as the whole of 2022 is 251 days. A wide window dilutes exactly what is being tested. So the definition is tightened in rungs: VIX at t−1 above its own trailing 67th, 90th and 95th percentile, which needs no dates and is available to a forecaster in real time, together with the named episodes at full width and as the 41 trading days centred on each window's VIX peak. The tightening was added after the wide version had been run, and we say so instead of presenting it as the original design.

The second decision matters more. Squared errors are larger when the target is more volatile, so a larger loss differential in stress can be nothing but heteroskedasticity. Every figure below is therefore the differential divided by the domestic model's own mean squared error inside the same regime: a share of error removed, comparable across states in a way the raw difference is not. The raw column is reported beside it, and the two tell different stories, and that is the reason for the precaution.

S11. Implied volatility in a stressed options market · No effect detected

The mirror objection to Section 7 is that redundancy given VIX and VDAX assumes a well-functioning options surface, and that during a crunch breadth might stop being redundant exactly when it matters. Applying the same ladder to the increment the foreign block adds on top of the implied-volatility model, across four delays and six states, the foreign block adds significantly in none of twenty-four cells, on the stressed rungs and the calm ones alike, down to the top 5% of VIX days. Redundancy given implied volatility is not an average concealing a stressed subsample. The severest rung carries 324 days, so an effect confined to conditions more extreme than the ladder reaches would not appear here.

One reading of that decay has to be ruled out before the rest is safe. If the foreign block worked by propagation, with volatility travelling from Tokyo to New York overnight, its value would be its earliness, the decay above would be the wave passing, and the right response would be a richer dynamic model, not a warning about feeds. The cross-correlations appear to speak to this and they are a trap. Correlating each peer at t−1 against the target at t, then the target at t−1 against the peer at t, all seven peers appear to LAG the target. But a row of this panel is not an instant. The target closes at 21:00 UTC and a peer closes c hours earlier, so the first direction spans 24 + c hours and the second 24 − c; for a persistent series the shorter span gives the higher correlation, and the clock alone predicts the observed sign whether or not anything leads anything.

That prediction is quantitative, so it can be tested instead of conceded. Each peer's asymmetry should scale with 2c. Across the seven it does, with a correlation of +0.93: 0.013 and 0.019 for the FTSE and the DAX, four and a half hours ahead of New York, against 0.051 for Bovespa, a full day ahead once admissibility has shifted it. The apparent lead–lag structure is a property of the calendar, not of the markets, and it is evidence for neither story. A check from the other side agrees: replacing the seven peers with the single real-time global factor of Section 6.1 and entering it at one lag at a time, the contemporaneous factor beats every lagged version in 26 of 30 cells. Yesterday's factor does add to today's at δ = 3 and δ = 5, and at no longer delay, so a little genuine dynamics is present, inside an otherwise contemporaneous story.

What is left is the reading Section S27 already arrived at by a different route. The block is a second reading of one shared state, it loses value as it ages at about the rate the first reading does, and it stops contributing once it is older than the mark it was brought in to replace: at δ = 13 a foreign block ten days stale returns the model to an effective age of 13.2 days, the domestic mark on its own. Breadth substitutes for a stale mark by being fresh, not by being early.

S13. An options market on another asset · Scope condition supported in the examined markets

Section 7 is the reason this paper's scope is narrower than its subject, and the narrowing turns on a word the section never examines. The implied-volatility block is VIX and VDAX. For an S&P target VIX is an options market written on the asset itself; for a DAX target VDAX is. For a Nikkei, Hang Seng, Nifty, ASX or Bovespa target both series belong to somebody else's index. Those are different purchases and the paper has been treating them as one.

The second case is also much closer to the case Section 1 opens with. A property book cannot buy an option on the building, because nobody writes one, and a quarterly-marked fund cannot buy an option on its own net asset value. Both can see the VIX every day for nothing, and Section S5 already leans on exactly that availability when it conditions on market state. So the question that decides how far Section 7 reaches is whether redundancy requires the option chain to be on your own asset, or whether anyone's options market will do.

It is answerable with the data already here. Each of the eight indices becomes the target in turn, with its own admissible cross-section under the clock rule of Section 4 and the same four models at each delay. Both implied-volatility series enter at t − 1 for every target, which is strictly admissible everywhere and identical across targets, so nothing differs between the two groups except whose options market the block contains.

One correction belongs here before those counts are read, and it changes them. Every increment above is measured with the seven-regressor block, which Sections 6.1 and S28 both say is not the model to run: seven coefficients cost one to five points of R², and on the S&P that cost was the whole of the negative increment given implied volatility. The bill is not the same size on every target, since it grows with delay and the eight targets differ in sample length and signal strength, so a cross-target comparison of net increments is partly a comparison of estimation costs. Giving every target the compressed block instead, the equal-weighted mean of its own admissible peers at a cost of one coefficient, removes that confound.

The split does not merely survive the correction, it sharpens. Where the options market is the asset's own, the mean increment rises from −0.0078 to +0.0029 and the count of significant cells stays at 0 of 8: compression removes the apparent harm and reveals nothing underneath it, which is Section S28's result on a second target. Where it belongs to another index, the mean increment rises from +0.0046 to +0.0153 and the count goes from 1 of 24 to 13 of 24, with a mean Giacomini-White statistic of 1.54. Over half of the cells in which a forecaster can see an options market but cannot buy one on what they hold are cells where the cross-section still pays, and the thirteenfold jump in that count from removing six coefficients is the section's result: what looked like redundancy on this group was mostly an estimation bill.

The gradient behind the split sharpens with it. Correlating each target's decision variable with the VIX block measures how close the only options market a forecaster can see sits to the thing they hold, and against the compressed increments that closeness correlates −0.78 across the eight targets, where the seven-regressor version read −0.38. Eight points is not a regression and no interval is quoted for it, but it is no longer a relationship a reader has to squint at.

The correction also disposes of the one target that did not fit. Bovespa looked like a market holding its own options chain, with the cross-section adding nothing and doing so significantly. It was paying for six coefficients it did not need: −0.0144 with seven regressors and +0.0098 with one. Two other explanations were available and both are ruled out rather than left open. The clock is not the cause, because entering the S&P same-day for a Bovespa forecaster, which this paper's own admissibility rule forbids and which is run only as a diagnostic, leaves the increment negative at all three delays. Nor is spanning: the partial correlation of the peer factor with the target given VIX, which is the quantity theory would reach for first, correlates −0.15 with the increment and predicts nothing. The lesson generalises past this table. A comparison of net increments across targets is partly a comparison of estimation bills, and on one target in eight that was enough to reverse the sign of the conclusion.

This does not reach the asset the paper is about, and one thing about the lower half has to be said before it is read as more than it is. Options trade on every target in it. Japan publishes the Nikkei Volatility Index, Hong Kong the VHSI, India the India VIX, Australia an S&P/ASX 200 VIX and Brazil an IVol-BR. What puts those targets in the lower half is that this paper holds VIX and VDAX and not those series, so the split is about the forecaster's information set and not about which option chains exist. That information set is the right analogy for Section 1's reader, who can see a public volatility index and cannot buy one written on what they hold, and it is not the same claim as an asset with no options at all. Section S14 supplies targets where the absence is a property of the market, and Section 11 keeps the gap rather than claiming it is closed.

S15. Clean lags against periodic marking · Approximate equivalence

Section 10 will argue that what carries from this paper to an illiquid asset is the apparatus rather than the numbers. One part of that claim can be checked rather than hoped for, and this section checks it. Every result here models staleness as a clean lag: the forecaster sees the state exactly as it stood δ days ago and nothing since. A marked asset is not stale in that way. It is revised on a schedule and held flat in between, and the revision is a partial adjustment toward current evidence, not a full mark, which is how the property literature has modelled appraisal indices for thirty years (Geltner et al. 2003). If the substitution rate under that kind of staleness looked nothing like the rate under a clean lag, R(δ) would be a curve about clean lags alone.

It does not. Rebuilding the domestic block as a mark revised every q days and held between revisions, and matching on mean staleness, gives 64% against the clean lag's 60% at ten days, 71% against 70% at thirty-one, and 70% against 72% at ninety-four. Adding partial adjustment on top moves it a few points the other way, 63% where matching predicts 70%, so the equivalence is approximate and we claim no more than that. One thing we got wrong on the way is worth recording: our first attempt modelled appraisal as pure exponential smoothing, which is not staleness at all. A weighted average of the recent past still contains today's observation, so it costs almost nothing. R² falls from 0.499 to 0.456 across a mean lag of eleven weeks, against 0.499 to −0.035 under a clean lag, and leaves almost no damage for breadth to repair. Holding a number flat between revisions is what makes a mark stale, not blurring it.

One detail of that definition needs stating, because it looks like look-ahead and is not. The target at+5 carries Mt+5, a median whose window includes the five days between the forecast origin and the outcome, which a forecaster at t does not know. S18. Does the house price result come from the index's own moving average? · Artefact explanation rejected, magnitude falls

Each published Case-Shiller figure for month t is a three-month moving average of sales pairs closing in months t − 2 to t. So consecutive monthly changes are built from overlapping transaction windows, every series in the panel carries the same induced persistence, and a reader is entitled to ask whether Section 8 recovers anything but that shared construction. The objection has to be answered with a design rather than a paragraph.

Where the overlap is and is not, stated precisely, because the answer is not what the objection assumes. At the headline delay of three months the target's own stale mark covers [t − 5, t − 3] and the fresh peer marks cover [t − 2, t]: disjoint, so no transaction month is shared between the stale mark and the cross-section replacing it. The overlap that does exist is between the peers' window and the label's, which reaches back to months t − 1 and t. A fresh peer mark carries conditions in those two months, and those months are inside the target's own next published change although nobody has published them for the target yet. That is what a stale mark is, so it is not obviously a defect; but it is also the channel through which a smoother could manufacture a rate, and argument cannot separate the two.

The design that can is to remove every shared transaction month. Read the index only every third month and the windows behind consecutive observations are disjoint, and so are the windows behind the stale mark, the peer block and the label. All three phases of the quarterly grid are run, because choosing one would leave the choice unexplained and the three are not the same sample. One quarter of relative staleness here is the same economic object as δ = 3 months in Section 8. Two estimators are reported, because on a sample a quarter the size a single metro whose own delay cost happens to be near zero dominates a mean of ratios; the pooled figure divides the average increment by the average delay cost and cannot be driven that way.

S19. The seam audit in full · No corruption detected

One join in the index data cannot be checked by comparing the two exports, and it is the S&P's: its files abut rather than overlap, so no row exists in both. That is the target series, and a silent corruption there would move every number in the paper. Section 10 states the limitation; this section removes the words "by comparison" from it, because Cboe publishes its own VIX history and that is a different vendor on a different data path, measuring the same underlying and spanning the join.

Three failures could occur in an export and each is visible to a different instrument, so three are used. A spliced block or a gross scale error breaks the relationship log σ²YZ = α + β log VIX, tested jointly on (α, β) by a Wald statistic with the same HAC correction used everywhere else here. A one-session date misalignment barely moves a five-day rolling variance and is invisible to that test, but it is glaring in the same-day relationship between the S&P's close-to-close return and the change in the exchange's index. A mis-scaled intraday range moves Yang-Zhang and leaves close-to-close variance alone, because 88% of the Yang-Zhang weight sits on a term the close never enters, so their ratio is an internal check needing no auditor at all. Each is calibrated against 400 pseudo-seams drawn from the S&P's own history and against eight dates where the S&P file has no join, which are the dates the other indices' files happen to meet and are therefore known-clean by construction.

At the join the three statistics sit at the 70th, 4th and 25th percentiles of their own nulls. The test is one-sided in what it can conclude, and the calibration shows why: several of the known-clean dates carry a large level statistic because they sit beside COVID, where the relationship between realised and implied volatility genuinely moved. A large statistic is therefore not proof of corruption, while a small one at the date the files meet is evidence that nothing happened to the data there. The power question is the one a limitation actually turns on, so it is answered by damaging the post-seam block deliberately and re-running all three: a one-session misalignment is caught, a block spliced in from the DAX is caught, and an intraday range mis-scaled by 25% is caught, while 10% is not. One corruption is invisible to all three and is named rather than left out: multiplying every post-seam price by a constant moves nothing, because all of this reads log ratios, and it is for the same reason the one corruption that could not change a number in this paper.

For VIX the concern is settled rather than bounded, because the two vendors' files can be compared directly. On the 6,747 dates both carry they are identical to the cent on 99.91% of rows and the mean absolute difference is 0.000222 index points; two rows fall outside this project's 0.5% seam tolerance, 28 November 2008 and 24 December 2012, both shortened sessions. The audit also found something we were not looking for. The retail export carries fifteen rows on dates United States equities were shut, 11 September 2001 among them: a fabricated observation rather than a discrepancy, and one no admissibility rule here could have caught, because the row is perfectly well formed. All fifteen fall outside the panel, so no model was ever shown one. VIX is Cboe's file throughout this paper, and substituting it moved no cell of Section 7 by as much as a thousandth of R². We report that as measurement instead of reassurance, and note that a retail export of any series can contain rows the exchange never produced.

The official file does not, however, supply the observation Section 4 actually wants, because it carries one open, high, low and close per day and no intraday snapshot at all. The 21:00 value of VIX existed and is the right regressor; it is not in any daily product, official or retail, and the lagged and same-day arms bracket it without recovering it. Differences at δ ≤ 2 fall inside the simultaneous bands. The variance-scale loss is dominated by a handful of crisis days, so its intervals are wide enough to be uninformative at short delays.

S20. The state-space alternative in full · Better estimator raises the rate

Section 10 names the estimator as the paper's largest limitation and reports the direction of the alternative. This section gives it. The competitor is one latent global volatility factor on an AR(1), every series loading on it, and a Kalman filter handling a missing observation by dropping that row from the measurement equation. That is what a delayed domestic mark is, rather than something imposed on the data, and it is the property that makes the comparison worth making at all: the filter is not merely a better regression, it is a model of the thing under study. It is fitted two-step after Doz, Giannone and Reichlin (2011), with the state-space parameters estimated once on the initial training block and then frozen, and the filtered state fed into the same forecasting ridge on the same days.

It beats the specification used throughout this paper at δ = 21 by 0.0119 of R², interval [+0.00021, +0.02382], and at δ = 55 by 0.0196, interval [+0.00316, +0.03530]. It loses at δ = 0, by 0.0224, interval [−0.03319, −0.00952]. The crossover is the informative part: imposing a one-factor structure costs skill when the mark is fresh and pays when it is stale, which is what a structural assumption should do when the data thin out. Its reconstruction of the current domestic state the forecaster is not allowed to see correlates 0.84 with the truth at eleven weeks, against 0.18 for the stale value itself.

What the result establishes is the size of what this paper's transparency costs: roughly two points of R² at the longest delay, which is larger than several of the effects the paper argues over. A bound in R² is not what a reader of this paper wants, so the headline itself was recomputed inside the filtered specification. The control cannot change, because Section S4 finds no filtered own-only model to build: with no observation noise in the domestic series the filtered state is the observation. So the filter enters through the model carrying the cross-section, where it is the better model at three of six delays, and the rate at eleven weeks becomes 75.4% against the published 71.6%, a paired difference of +3.8% [+0.8%, +7.7%]. In days, the mark reads 3.7 days old [2.0, 6.7] rather than the published 4.6, a paired difference of −0.9 days [−2.3, −0.2]. The interval on 3.7 is that file's own bootstrap and is not the one Section S12 quotes beside 4.6, which is why the paired difference rather than the pair of intervals is the number to read.

The estimator limitation therefore does not run the way a limitation usually does. A better estimator raises the numerator of R(δ) and has nothing to give its denominator, so the paper is not protecting a large number with a transparent estimator; it is quoting a smaller one than the better estimator would give it. What is still declined is the rest of the paper inside a filtered specification, which is a different paper: R(δ) is a ratio in which the same domestic control appears three times, so a better estimator raises numerator and denominator together and the rate is not simply the rate plus the improvement.

S21. The estimation bill under an unfitted generator · Objection answered

Both measurements of the bill are relative to something. The slope method extrapolates from peer counts; the surrogate method replaces the foreign block with fitted Gaussian AR(1) series. One objection to the second is correct: it preserves first-order persistence and contemporaneous cross-market covariance and nothing else: not heavy tails, not volatility-of-volatility, not crisis clustering, not higher-order serial structure. Under that objection the cost is identified only relative to a generator we chose, and a forecaster's real regressors are not Gaussian AR(1) series.

The objection has a direct test. Measure the bill again with a generator that fits nothing at all: take the real foreign block and move it circularly through the sample. Every marginal, every tail, every cluster is then the real one, and the only thing destroyed is the alignment with the target. If an unfitted generator returns the fitted generator's number, the objection is answered, not conceded.

The random first layer is not learned, and a learned one has strictly more free parameters, so it faces the same estimation-cost bill this sample has repeatedly failed to pay, which is an argument by extension rather than a test. Section 10 records it as such.

S25. The two variance-scale losses, and why only one of them is Patton's · Claim corrected, conclusion unaffected

One correction belongs here rather than in a footnote, because an earlier version of this paper got it wrong in a way that flattered the result. That version reconstructed nothing: it computed squared error on the median-normalised variance, exp(yt) against γt exp(ft), which is Patton's MSE weighted by 1 / M² whenever both sides are reconstructed with the same median. The weight is largest exactly where the median is smallest, so it is not a neutral rescaling, and the two losses are not the same loss: the substitution rate under the normalised version runs 6 to 29 points above the natural-scale one, by 10 points at eleven weeks. A forecaster cannot reconstruct both sides with the same median, because the outcome carries Mt+h and only Mt is known at the origin, so that gap is the weight plus the median's drift over h days rather than the weight alone. The table below splits it with a third, infeasible column and finds the drift worth at most 1.1 points at any delay, which leaves the weight carrying essentially all of the correction.

Mean absolute error, used in some recent work in this area, is in no version of the family. One choice inside that arrangement needs defending, because it looks backwards: the headline number in this paper is the one loss of the three that is NOT proxy-robust. We lead with log-scale R² because the paper's object is a ratio, R(δ), and a ratio needs a denominator a reader can hold: the share of a delay-induced loss recovered, naturally bounded near 0 and 100%, on the same scale as the companion note (Vellanikaran 2026) and as the decision variable itself. QLIKE skill on a variance scale is the right loss and the wrong ruler for that purpose; its levels are not comparable to anything else in this literature. The defence is that the choice is presentational and is made where it can be checked: both proxy-robust losses are reported beside the headline in every table where a conclusion turns on one, and they support the same conclusions everywhere including the δ = 0 row, where both point in the same negative direction but the natural-scale MSE is too imprecise to establish an effect. Because the magnitude there is about one point of R² and the practical question concerns stale rather than current marks, no economic claim in this paper rests on that row, and Section S9 of the Internet Appendix says so. A reader who prefers the robust family throughout can read the QLIKE columns and will reach the same conclusions with different numbers attached.

Section 4.4 reports squared error on the natural variance scale and says that an earlier version of this paper computed it on the median-normalised scale instead while calling it Patton's MSE. This section gives both columns, because the size of that mistake is a fact about the paper and should be on the record as a number rather than as an admission.

Two things separate the two columns, and only the first of them is a factor. The target is yt = log(σ²YZ,t+h / Mt+h), so exp(yt) is a variance divided by a median and exp(yt) Mt+h is the variance itself. Had the forecast been reconstructed with that same median, squared error on the normalised pair would be squared error on the natural pair weighted by exactly 1 / M²t+h, and nothing else would differ. But Mt+h is not known at t. The only reconstruction a forecaster can run multiplies the forecast by Mt, and the natural-scale column reported throughout this paper is that one, so moving from the normalised column to it takes the weight off and moves the forecast's median h days at the same time. The gap between those two columns is therefore not, on its own, a measurement of the weight, and an earlier version of this section said it was.

Table S20 separates them with a third column nobody could compute. Reconstruct both sides with Mt+h: per day that loss is exactly M²t+h times the normalised loss, which the script checks numerically rather than asserting, to 1.6e−12 in relative terms. So what separates that column from the normalised one is the weight alone, and what separates it from the implementable one is the median's h-day drift alone. The weight turns out to carry nearly all of the correction, 28.8 of the 29.4 points at one day and 9.0 of the 10.1 at eleven weeks, with the drift never worth more than 1.1 points at any delay, so the earlier wording had the economics right and the algebra wrong, and what is on the record now is two named effects rather than one undifferentiated number.

The weight is not innocuous: it is largest in the calmest windows, which is exactly where the delay does least damage and the substitution rate's denominator is smallest. Patton's result is about the unweighted loss (2011). QLIKE is unaffected by any of this, and not by luck: it depends on the ratio of outcome to forecast, so a common factor cancels exactly, which Part 0 of the script demonstrates numerically to ten decimal places rather than asserting.

S26. A model of the delay curve

This section states a specification before the measurements that support it, because written down it turns the delay curve from a description into a prediction with a testable ceiling, and that ceiling is what Sections 5 to 7 are then measured against. The specification is not assumed: it is the shape two of those measurements take, and a reader who prefers the evidence first can read Sections 5 and 6 and return here.

Section S27 finds the recovery entirely in the leading component of the foreign block, with the six orthogonal components worth less than nothing. Section S4 of the Internet Appendix finds that the level of volatility, which equation (2) removes, carries information that grows with delay. Together they say: log variance is a slow level, plus a factor several markets share, plus something idiosyncratic. For market i,

log σ²i,t = μi,t + λi gt + ei,t,     gt = φ gt−1 + ut,(10)

with μ the slow level the 252-day median tracks, g a persistent global factor, e idiosyncratic and u white noise. The decision variable of equation (2) subtracts an estimate of μ, so ai,t ≈ λi gt + ei,t. That is the whole of Section S4 of the Internet Appendix in one line: the normalisation removes μ by construction, so restoring it is worth something.

A forecaster δ days late sees aSPX,t−δ, one noisy reading of gt−δ. The cross-section supplies seven readings of gt, whose common component suppresses the idiosyncratic part. Under (10),

E[ gt+h | gt−δ ] = φ h+δ gt−δ,     E[ gt+h | gt ] = φ h gt,(11)

and three consequences follow, each checkable against quantities measured elsewhere in this paper, not fitted to the curve.

First, the stale forecaster's skill decays geometrically: log Sown(δ) is linear in δ with slope 2 log φ. Fitted over the eight delays where Sown is positive, the line has R² = 0.9980 and slope -0.05419 per day, implying φ = 0.9733. The factor's own persistence, estimated from the principal-component series and never shown the decay curve, is 0.9678. The two agree to 0.0055. The delay curve is the factor's memory, seen through a forecast.

Second, Scross(δ) should be near-flat, because the foreign block delivers gt whatever the domestic delay is. Across eleven weeks it falls 27%, against 99% for the domestic model. Perfect flatness is not predicted, because the domestic block is still in the cross-sectional model and still decays, but the contrast is the model's.

Third, and most useful: R(δ) rises from zero towards a ceiling below one, and the model names it. Since Sown(δ) → 0,

R(∞) = Scross ÷ Sown(0),(12)

which is below one because seven peers estimate gt with error and never observe eSPX,t. Both quantities are measured elsewhere: Scross(55) = 0.3736 and Sown(0) = 0.5191, giving a ceiling of 72.0%, against a measured 69.8% at δ = 34 and 71.6% at δ = 55.

That agreement is weaker evidence than an earlier version of this section claimed, and the reason is arithmetic rather than empirical. Write the rate out: R(δ) = [Scross(δ) − Sown(δ)] ÷ [Sown(0) − Sown(δ)]. When Sown(δ) is near zero (at δ = 55 it is 0.0070), this collapses to Scross(55) ÷ Sown(0), which is equation (12). So the “prediction” and the measurement are built from the same two numbers and must agree; the 0.4-point gap between 71.97% and 71.59% is the size of Sown(55), not the accuracy of a forecast. This is sharper now than it was: before the benchmark correction of Section 4.4, Sown(55) was −0.0351 and the same two quantities differed by 1.8 points.

We therefore call (12) a factor-model interpretation of the observed plateau rather than a prediction of it. What the model earns is the reading: the curve flattens because the cross-section's skill is a factor share bounded by how well seven peers estimate g, and the level it flattens at is that share. What it does not earn is credit for having predicted a number it is algebraically tied to. A genuine out-of-sample test would estimate the ceiling from parameters independent of the endpoint being predicted (the factor's loadings and its persistence, say, rather than Scross(55) itself), and this paper has not done that.

So the substitution rate is not an arbitrary rising function. It is a factor share, it converges, and the paper can say what it converges to. The model also explains why Section S27's orthogonal remainder is worthless, being e, which forecasts nothing, and why Section S12 of the Internet Appendix's staleness penalty exists, since an aged foreign block reads g at t − g, not at t and (11) prices that at φ per day. Three results the paper had separately are one mechanism.

What the model does not explain is why the mechanism is stronger in stress. Equation (11) holds λ and φ constant; Section S5 of the Internet Appendix shows the factor doing more work when markets are frightened, which would need λ or the variance of u to move with state. We have not fitted that, and it is the obvious next specification.

S27. Breadth against its own leading component

A further objection is omitted-variable bias: foreign breadth may not be capturing cross-border diffusion at all, but proxying for a global systemic risk factor, so that what looks like transmission is simultaneous reaction to shared news. Section 6.1 is most of the way to agreeing, since one real-time factor beats all seven series at every delay, but it never separates the two halves and asks which one carries the recovery. The decomposition is available at no cost, because the basis is already being estimated.

At each refit, write v1 for the leading eigenvector of the training covariance of the standardised foreign block. pt = gt v1 + rt,     gt = pt′v1,     rt = pt − gt v1,(13)

where gt is the scalar global factor and rt is the six-dimensional remainder orthogonal to v1. Sign pinning is not cosmetic: an eigenvector is defined only up to sign, and one that flipped between refits would enter the regression as noise unrelated to the data. It records a failure to distinguish, not a demonstration of equality: the widest of the four intervals above rules out a gross gain larger than 0.019 of R², at δ = 21 and leaves anything smaller open, so what is established is a bound and not a zero. The table above is the admissible specification: VIX lagged a full day, which discards a day of information the live index would have carried. Neither arm is the specification we would choose, and the one we would choose is not available to us. What licenses a conclusion here is not that the 21:00 observation falls between these two in time, which would say nothing about how it forecasts, but that the verdict is the same under a specification that gives the options market a full day less information and under one that gives it fifteen minutes more. A finding that survives that much variation in the input is not resting on the timing choice.

One feature of that result deserves a sentence, because it is more striking than it first looks. VIX is a thirty-calendar-day implied volatility and the target here is realised variance over five trading days, and the two are not in the same unit: thirty calendar days is about twenty-one trading days, so the winning instrument is measured over a horizon about four times the length of the thing it is forecasting, not six. That it dominates anyway says something about how much of the five-day question is answered by the level of the whole term structure, not by its front. The matched instrument exists, since Cboe publishes a nine-day index, VIX9D, formerly VXST, and Section S24 runs the race again with it. The horizon does matter, by rather more than we expected, and the conclusion of this section does not.

Three further questions about this race are answered below: whether redundancy survives compressing the cross-section, what the skill column means economically, and three ways the race could have been unfair to breadth.

S28.1 One regressor instead of seven

Section 6 says that cost is real and Section 6.1 says a compression avoids part of it, so the confound is not hypothetical. The compression supplies the fix: add one regressor instead of seven.

8.2 Economic interpretation of the skill column

A point of R² on log variance is not an intuitive quantity, and the reading a risk manager needs is narrower than the column suggests. What breadth repairs is a stale forecaster's tendency to over-estimate variance; the understating tail is unmoved: it runs between 8.6% and 11.4% of days whatever the delay and whichever model is used. Concretely, at eleven weeks the stale domestic forecaster's variance forecast is off by a median factor of 1.90, and is more than a factor of two out on 59.2% of days against 37.1% for the forecaster holding the cross-section, against 31.1% for a forecaster with no delay at all. Breadth closes most of that gap, and all of what it closes is over-estimation. Anyone reaching for the cross-section as protection against being surprised by a volatility spike is reaching for the wrong instrument.

8.3 Three ways the race could be unfair

Three objections to the comparison above can each be tested rather than argued about, and all three are tested in the Internet Appendix so that this section stays the length of its result. None of them overturns it.

Redundancy was established with a ridge, and a linear map might simply be too blunt to find what the foreign block holds given implied volatility. Section S22 of the Internet Appendix gives the block two richer maps: a random Fourier feature expansion at two widths, which is a one-hidden-layer network whose first layer is drawn rather than learned, and a k-nearest-neighbour arm imposing no functional form at all. Nothing appears. Every statistic that would have overturned the redundancy result comes back negative, and the richer maps cost more than the plain block does.

Every number above forecasts five trading days ahead, and Section S23 of the Internet Appendix re-runs the whole experiment at one day, two weeks and one month to establish that this is a convention rather than a finding. The rate is stable across the middle of that range and falls at the ends for reasons the section separates: at one day the target is an object the forecaster can largely already see, and at one month it has dropped to 51% with an interval too wide to lean on. Section 10 treats that as a limit on how far the result reaches rather than as a result.

One asymmetry in the race is worth removing rather than defending. The target is realised variance five days ahead and VIX prices a thirty-day horizon, so the section above ran the options arm with a horse suited to a different distance. Cboe's VIX9D is the closest listed instrument, with a horizon about a third of VIX's and near-matched to the target rather than exactly matched, and Section S24 of the Internet Appendix runs the race again with it, giving both blocks the same number of regressors so that the estimation cost of Section 6.1 cancels in the difference. The horizon objection is correct and matching it is worth something measurable; the conclusion of Section 7 survives it, and the section states plainly what the shorter sample the test requires costs.

S29. Feasibility at the asset's reporting frequency

The larger absence is the one Sections 8 and S30 keep naming, and this paper has treated it as though the difficulty were finding an appraisal-based series. That is not the difficulty, and being precise about what is changes what a future study should do. The proxy is Yang-Zhang over five daily bars, which needs an open, a high, a low and a close for every session, and an appraised asset has none of those: it has one number per quarter. The walk-forward then needs 252 + 1,250 + 250 = 1,752 observations before its first forecast, which at one mark per quarter is four hundred and thirty-eight years. No dataset solves that. The design has to be re-specified at the frequency the asset reports, and the question nobody had asked is whether the measurement survives when it is.

It does. Coarsening the S&P and its seven peers to month-end marks throws away exactly what an appraised series never had, leaving the proxy to be built from the variance of the last six monthly returns against a sixty-month median, with the delay counted in months and every window scaled by twenty-one. On 109 test months the rate at a one-quarter delay is 49% [34, 76], and at six months 55% [47, 75]. Smoothing the mark by the partial adjustment of Section S15 of the Internet Appendix, which is how the property literature has described appraisal indices for thirty years, leaves 44% [28, 75] and 54% [43, 79].

S30. What a practitioner should do with this

Someone holding a stale mark on an asset with a liquid options market should prefer own-asset implied volatility for this forecasting objective, at the tenor matching their forecast horizon rather than at the headline one. Section 7 provides strong evidence for the first, and the foreign cross-section adds nothing measurable once implied volatility is in hand. Section S28 provides it for the second: for a five-day question the nine-day index beats thirty-day VIX at every delay we test, by 0.024 to 0.041 of R², which is a larger prize than most of what the rest of this paper argues over and costs nothing but choosing the right series.

Someone holding a stale mark on an asset with no options market has breadth as the only candidate on the table, and it is no corner case: it is the case Section 1 opens with. Section S13 of the Internet Appendix puts a measurement behind that sentence for the first time. Where the only options market a forecaster can see is written on another index, implied volatility and breadth come out three points apart rather than thirty-one, and a compressed cross-section adds significantly in thirteen of twenty-four cells against none of eight where the option chain is on the asset itself. Buying someone else's options market is not the substitute that buying your own is. Quarterly-marked private equity, appraised property and funds reporting a lagged net asset value have no listed option chain on the asset itself, so the substitute that wins Section 7 is not available to any of them. For appraised property the number no longer has to be extrapolated from equities at all: Section 8 measures it on twenty metropolitan house price indices and finds 67.8% [45, 92] of the loss from a quarter's extra staleness recovered on the return and 19.0% [6, 31] on the variance, with the peer cross-section compressed to a single column, which is the same recommendation the rest of this section makes for a different reason. The redundancy result therefore narrows where the equity measurement transfers, not where the question matters. Before buying anything, though, there is a cheaper thing to do, and Section S14 of the Internet Appendix is what makes it worth saying. Correlate the stale series with the public cross-section over the days already in hand. Across eight markets that one number tracks the substitution rate at +0.98, it runs from 0.81 down to −0.12 across targets that all look like ordinary equity indices, and where it is small the cross-section repaired nothing and charged for the attempt. A holder whose asset is weakly coupled to public markets should expect breadth to do nothing for them, and can learn that before they buy a feed rather than after. Sections 4 and 5 show how that worth can be measured and give a liquid-market benchmark for it, rather than an estimate for the illiquid asset itself: about 80% of the delay-induced loss recovered in stressed markets, where the estimate is stable and tight, 81% at eleven weeks with an interval of [74, 87], and an amount this sample cannot determine in calm ones. One practical point belongs with those numbers and is easy to miss, because the paper reports it as a robustness result. The rates above come from the original seven-regressor specification, which is not the model to run. Section 6.1 shows a single real-time factor beating all seven series at every delay and raising R(55) to 75.2%, and an equal-weighted mean of the seven does nearly as well for no estimation at all. The seven coefficients cost one to five points of R² to fit and buy nothing back. Anyone building this should compress the cross-section first and treat the seven-regressor figures as the conservative benchmark they are. The unconditional figure is 72% against the paper's own control and 68% against the stronger control of Section S4 of the Internet Appendix; we quote the conditional numbers because they are the ones that mean anything. Against that sits a cost of one to five points of R² for carrying the extra series. One condition comes with those figures, and Section S12 of the Internet Appendix makes it explicit: the foreign data has to be current. Every rate quoted in this paper assumes the cross-section arrives the same evening, and a feed one day behind turns Table S8 of the Internet Appendix's 36% into 9% at three days and its 51% into 29% at five. Before buying breadth, check that it will arrive on time. A cross-section older than the mark it is replacing is worth nothing, and at the shortest delays it is worth less than nothing.

One generalisation is worth refusing explicitly. It would be convenient to present Figure 2 as a benchmark for how quickly information decays in delayed systems, and it is not one. Those curves are a measurement of one index against seven specific peers over one sample. The correlation between a private portfolio and the public cross-section is a different quantity, and nothing here bounds it. A reader who wants a number for their own asset will not find it in this paper.

What does carry across is the apparatus rather than the numbers. R(δ) is defined in units that survive a change of loss function, market or candidate. The delay sweep is a design anyone with a delayed series and a candidate substitute can run, and the horse race in Section 7 is the question to ask first, because its answer decides whether breadth is worth buying at all. The cost decomposition in Section 6 separates what a source knows from what a forecaster keeps, the distinction that turned our own most confusing result into an ordinary one. Pointed at an appraisal-based or quarterly-marked series, that machinery would produce the numbers this paper cannot, and the check above says the delay model it carries is the right shape for that series, even though the numbers are not.

S31.2 Twenty metropolitan house price indices

Three supporting measurements are held back to the end of this section: the panel's properties measured before any rate was estimated, the full pricing of the three candidate peer blocks, and the seasonal placebo.

Two things change from Section 4 and both are worth stating plainly, because a result that moved two variables at once and reported one of them would not be worth much. The asset class changes. The target variable also changes, because a monthly index has no intraday range and Yang-Zhang cannot be computed on it at all. The two are therefore carried separately throughout. One target is next month's log return, which is the quantity an owner of the asset cares about and is not this paper's target. The other is the log of trailing twelve-month realised variance over its own five-year median. That second target needs a caveat rather than a claim of equivalence. This paper's own target is forward-looking: realised variance over the five days after the forecast origin, none of which the forecaster has seen. The housing one is backward-looking, and forecasting next month's value of it means forecasting a quantity whose window overlaps the current one in eleven of twelve months. An own-only R² near 0.95 follows from that arithmetic and is not evidence that house price variance is easy to forecast. It also shrinks the denominator R(δ) divides by, which is why the rates on this coordinate are lower and why we do not read them as the same measurement made in a second asset class. The return target is the closer analogue of the paper's, and where the two coordinates are given different weight below, that is the reason.

Delta needs saying carefully too. All twenty metros carry the same publication lag of roughly two months, so delta is not that lag. It is what it is everywhere else in this paper: the amount by which the target's own mark is staler than the cross-section it sits in. The concrete case is an owner of property in one metro whose book is appraised on a cycle running behind the published index for the others. Three months is one quarterly appraisal cycle and is the row to read first.

The peer block has to be chosen before any rate is read, and at this sample size the choice is not cosmetic. Section S28 and Section S30 already recommend compressing the cross-section to a single real-time factor rather than fitting seven separate series, for the reason Section 6 prices. The bill is far larger here: the training window is 144 months rather than 1,250 days, and on the variance target the own-only model already reaches an R² of 0.9497, so thirteen peer columns have almost nothing left to earn and a great deal to spend. Table S27 prices three blocks as a set, the way Section S4 of the Internet Appendix prices eight controls, and selects one by a rule fixed in advance: take the block with the highest cross-arm skill at zero delay, treat differences below 0.01 as immaterial, and break ties toward the smaller block. That statistic is measured where the own mark is fresh and there is nothing to substitute for, so it cannot favour a high rate, and the tie-break runs toward fewer regressors, which is the direction that lowers it.

The first objection is the index's own construction. Each published figure for month t is a three-month moving average of sales pairs closing in months t − 2 to t, so consecutive monthly changes are built from overlapping transaction windows, and a rate manufactured by that smoother would look exactly like a rate. Whether that holds outside a repeat-sales index is not something fourteen metros can establish.

What this section settles, and what it does not. It settles that the central claim of this paper is not a fact about liquid equity indices. The claim holds on an asset class with no intraday range, no option chain and a mark that is smoothed by construction; it holds on the target variable the asset's owner cares about and on the one this paper was written about; it holds under a peer block chosen by a rule that could not see the rate; and it is not a calendar effect. What it does not do is price the effect more tightly than the intervals in Table S25, or test the slope of the coupling relation, for the reason given before the rates were estimated. And it remains an index study: nobody holds a Case-Shiller index, and a real appraised book would differ from one in ways this panel cannot show. What is no longer true is the sentence this paper carried until now, that the illiquid case rests on argument alone. It now rests on a measurement one step removed from the asset the argument is about.

S31.1 The panel, the peer blocks and the seasonal placebo

Section S29 established that the design survives being re-specified at monthly frequency, and closed by saying what was then missing: a series with two to four decades of marks and a public cross-section, run at the asset's own frequency. That series exists and it is free. The S&P Cotality Case-Shiller indices cover twenty United States metropolitan areas to June 2026, 9,143 published figures in all. Fourteen of them run monthly from January 1987; Minneapolis and Phoenix begin in 1989, Seattle in 1990, Atlanta and Detroit in 1991, and Dallas not until 2000. Rates are estimated on the fourteen with the full history, which is why a panel of twenty is reported as fourteen of fourteen, and the six later starters enter as peers wherever they exist. Section 9 widens the peer block to all nineteen. These indices are the object this paper has spent nine sections arguing about rather than a proxy for it. Each monthly figure is a three-month moving average of closings and closings lag contract by weeks, so the mark is smoothed and stale before anyone touches it. No option chain is written on any of the twenty, so the implied-volatility substitute that dominates Section 7 is not merely weak here, it is absent. And all twenty publish on the same day, so when one is treated as stale, nineteen fresh peer marks exist: the construction of Section 4, transplanted whole.

Before any rate was estimated, two properties of the panel were measured, and both bear on claims made earlier in this paper. The first is persistence. Section S15 of the Internet Appendix models a stale mark as partial adjustment at rho = 0.6, a figure taken from the property literature, and the monthly return autocorrelation of the real indices runs from 0.601 at Cleveland to 0.939 at Phoenix with a mean of 0.804. Every metro is more smoothed than the model of it, so the synthetic illiquid case of Section S29 was a conservative stand-in and not a flattering one. The second is coupling, which Section S14 of the Internet Appendix found orders the substitution rate at +0.98 across eight equity targets. Here it spans 0.662 to 0.866 with a mean of 0.763, against 0.228 to 0.808 in Section S14 of the Internet Appendix. That has one consequence in each direction and both were committed to before the rates existed. The level prediction is sharp and testable: every metro sits at or near the top of the equity coupling range, so the mechanism predicts a high rate on all of them and no target on which substitution fails. The slope prediction is not testable here at all, because a band 0.35 times the width of the one that produced +0.98 cannot resolve a gradient, and claiming the slope held on this panel would be the same error as claiming it failed.

Widening the cross-section from thirteen peers to nineteen, six of which begin between 1989 and 2000 and are therefore genuinely ragged, moves the rate from 67.8% to 67.5% on the return target and from 19.0% to 19.9% on the variance target. Nothing is lost, which is what compressing to a mean is for, and nothing is gained either, which is the more informative half. Six additional metros move the rate by less than a point, so thirteen peers already span whatever common factor the cross-section carries: that is the prediction of Section S27 and of the factor model in Section S8 of the Internet Appendix holding on a second asset class, and it is of a piece with seasonal profiles that correlate +0.894 and couplings that sit in a band 0.2 wide. There is one national housing cycle in this panel, not nineteen independent signals.

S32. Robustness and scope in full

Read as a clock, the trade has a price. A fifty-five-day-old domestic mark plus today's foreign closes forecasts as well as a plain domestic mark 4.6 days old [2.5, 8.4]. The same construction prices the other side of the trade: one day of staleness in the foreign feed costs 2.1 days [1.4, 3.3] of domestic freshness, and a feed one day behind turns the 51% recovered at five days into 29%, and the 36% at three days into 9%. Every rate in this paper assumes the cross-section arrives the same evening, and a cross-section older than the mark it replaces is worth less than nothing.

S33. The limitations in full

The implied-volatility block is thinner than we would like. VIX and VDAX cover the sample; a spot VSTOXX index was obtained, tested and kept out of the headline block, because requiring it costs 73% of the sample, moving the window from 4,525 days beginning September 2008 to 1,240 beginning April 2020: the 2008 crisis is discarded outright and peak VIX falls from 82.7 over the full sample to 41.4 inside what remains. On the 1,240 days where all three exist, holding those days fixed so the third series is not confounded with the shorter and calmer window, it adds nothing at any delay and is mildly negative at all six: VSTOXX and VDAX are both European equity volatility indices and the extra column costs more to estimate than it returns. The conclusion a fuller block was meant to test is therefore unaffected. What remains open is the export rather than the conclusion, since it runs only from December 2012 to March 2025.

Two bounds on the tests themselves. The non-linearity result of Section S28 fits a random Fourier layer, which is a one-hidden-layer network whose first layer is drawn rather than learned; no network with a learned first layer was fitted, and the argument that one would not change the answer is an extension of the estimation-cost finding rather than a test of it. And Section S27's first principal component of seven equity indices is a proxy for global systemic risk, not a measurement of it, so the remainder it leaves may still carry global content this basis cannot see: the direction of that test is informative and its magnitude is not a bound. Section S28 names a limitation of a different kind, and it is the one a practitioner should read first. What breadth repairs is a stale forecaster's tendency to over-estimate variance, not their exposure to under-estimating it; the understating tail is unmoved at every delay we checked. Anyone reaching for the cross-section as protection against being caught short by a volatility spike is reaching for the wrong thing.

The largest of these limits is the estimator, and we can be specific about it rather than gesturing, because we fitted the alternative. Every model here is a ridge on a fixed feature set, chosen because it is transparent and because the object under study is the substitution rate rather than the best attainable forecast. The natural competitor is a one-factor state-space model with a Kalman filter that handles a missing observation by dropping that row from the measurement equation, which is what a delayed domestic mark is rather than something imposed on the data. Fitted two-step after Doz, Giannone and Reichlin (2011), it beats this paper's specification at δ = 21 and δ = 55 and loses at δ = 0, and the crossover is the interesting part: imposing a one-factor structure costs skill when the mark is fresh and pays when it is stale. Section S20 of the Internet Appendix gives the intervals and the reconstruction diagnostics.

We report it rather than adopting it, and the reason is a scope judgement a reader is entitled to disagree with: R(δ) is a ratio in which the same domestic control appears three times, so a better estimator raises numerator and denominator together, and recomputing every conditional and every robustness check inside a filtered specification is a different paper. What matters for the headline is that the direction runs the opposite way to the usual limitation. Inside the filtered specification the rate at eleven weeks becomes 75.4% against the published 71.6%, a paired difference of +3.8% [+0.8%, +7.7%], and the mark reads 3.7 days old [2.0, 6.7] rather than 4.6. A better estimator raises the numerator of R(δ) and has nothing to give its denominator, so this paper is not protecting a large number with a transparent estimator; it is quoting a smaller one than the better estimator would give it. The 75.4% is the upper end of the 68% to 75% span the abstract reports.

S34. Further work in full

The way it breaks is the more interesting outcome, and it is specific enough to look for. Commodity volatility has drivers that no equity cross-section can see: an inventory report, a harvest, a production decision by a cartel. If a commodity block adds where equity breadth does not, then “a global volatility factor seen early” is too coarse a description of what is happening, and the right cross-section depends on what the stale asset IS, and is not one panel for everyone. A farmland book and a private credit book would then need different peers, which is a materially different recommendation from the one Section S30 gives and would be worth the trouble of discovering.

Two warnings for whoever runs it. The models are the easy part; the data hygiene is not. Futures require contract rolls, so a continuous series carries a seam every quarter instead of the single seam per export audited in Section 4, and the audit, not the estimation, is where that work will actually go. And the admissibility table stops being a list of closing times: a bond future and its underlying cash market can trade on different calendars, so the rule that a peer counts only when its timestamp precedes the target's needs restating instrument by instrument instead of country by country.

S35. The admissibility rule on real session clocks

Section 3 states the rule the whole design rests on: a foreign close is admissible on day t only if its timestamp falls strictly before the target's close on that day. The rule is implemented from a table of fixed UTC closing times, and those are standard-time closes. Real exchanges keep local time, most of them observe daylight saving, and they do not switch on the same dates: the United States advances its clocks on the second Sunday in March and the European Union on the last Sunday, so for a fortnight each spring New York sits an hour closer to London than the table says. Until now the paper asserted that this never reorders a block. An assertion about a clock is a poor substitute for reading one, and this section reads them.

Every close in the panel is rebuilt in UTC from its exchange's own regular-session closing time in its own timezone, with the IANA database supplying every transition date, on all 7021 dates in the sample. Two decisions are then compared, for every target, every peer and every date: the lag the fixed table assigns, and the lag the true closes imply. A disagreement in one direction is a leak, where the table calls a peer same-day admissible and the clock says it closed later or at the same moment, so the panel would carry a value the forecaster could not have seen. A disagreement in the other is waste, where the table sends a peer back a day that had in fact already closed.

There are 0 leaks. Across every target, every peer and every date, not one day on which the true session clocks would have reordered a block. The margin is not marginal either: the narrowest gap the rule anywhere relies on is 2.00 hours, between N225 and HSI, so the fixed table is a simplification of the clock rather than an approximation of the rule. Daylight saving moves four of the eight closes by exactly one hour and moves none of them past a target.

The waste runs the other way and is worth stating because of its direction. On 3345 of the 7,021 dates the table sends AXJO back a day for a N225 forecaster when the true clocks say it had already closed, and 6099 such days occur across the panel as a whole. The cause is that the table records the two markets at the same standard-time hour and the rule resolves equality against admissibility. So the implementation is stricter than the rule it implements: it discards information a real forecaster could lawfully have used. That direction is the one to prefer, and it also changes how the paper's numbers should be read. Every rate reported here is what a forecaster achieves with less than the admissible cross-section, which makes the measured substitution rate a lower bound on the rule's own terms rather than a figure flattered by a generous clock.

Holidays are the second assumption in the same family and they cannot leak, since a peer whose exchange was shut carries a close that is strictly older than the day it sits on. What they can do is weaken the instrument, so the frequency is reported rather than assumed: the peer carried forward most often is N225, on 5.8% of the target's days, and the mean age of a carried close is under three days. The foreign block is a block of recent closes, not a block of stale ones.

S36. The outage, split into information and estimation

Section 4.3 names what δ freezes and points here for the measurement. The training cut of equation (4) is forced by the vintage assumption: the label is the target's own decision variable, so a forecaster whose own mark is δ days old has not seen the outcomes that resolved since. The consequence, which an external audit put more sharply than an earlier objection had, is that δ does not isolate a stale input. It freezes the current domestic state, the labels available for fitting, the coefficient vintage, the ridge penalty the validation tail selects, and the quantity of training data, all at once. The design measures a domestic-data outage, and the question this section answers is how much of the damage belongs to each half.

The instrument matters and it is chosen rather than defaulted to. Bonferroni controls the probability of any false rejection at all and is the right test if a single cell is to be leaned on; Benjamini-Hochberg controls the expected share of rejections that are false and is the right test for a grid read as a pattern, which is how these grids are actually read. We report both and lean on neither individually: the claim these grids support is the pattern, which survives, and not any one of its cells, which mostly does not.

Nothing here touches the headline, and the exclusion is deliberate rather than convenient. R(55) on the implementable target is a single pre-specified comparison, its multiplicity is priced in Section S18 against the nine specifications actually searched, and it survives Reality Check and SPA at five of six delays. Pooling it into a set of two hundred secondary cells would not be a stricter test of it; it would be a different and weaker one, in which a grid the headline was never part of decides the threshold the headline must clear.
