What Substitutes for a Stale Mark? Breadth, Implied
Volatility, and the Price of Being Late

Sunny Alex Vellanikaran

Independent

16 September 2026

Abstract

A forecaster whose own data has gone stale must buy something in place of timeliness, and there is no accepted way to price that trade. Handicapping a forecaster of S&P 500 realised variance by δ days while every other source stays current, we define the substitution rate R(δ), the share of the delay's damage a candidate substitute repairs, and price two candidates against it over nineteen years.

The result is most legible in days. A fifty-five-day-old mark plus today's foreign index closes forecasts as well as a mark 4.6 days old [2.8, 7.7]; one day of staleness in that foreign feed costs 2.1 days [1.4, 3.3] of domestic freshness. Three conditions bound it. The rate is well determined only in stressed markets, where it is 81% [74, 87] at eleven weeks and identical in both halves of the sample; in calm markets so little damage is done that the ratio is undetermined. Against the strongest domestic control we could build it is 68% rather than 72%. And where the asset carries a liquid options market the cross-section is redundant, and that survives replacing thirty-day VIX with a horizon-matched nine-day index that forecasts better at every delay. That bounds the scope of the finding, not the method: quarterly-marked private equity, appraised property and lagged-NAV funds have no option chain to buy instead.

Two practical results sit inside the measurement. Fitting seven foreign series is the wrong way to buy breadth: one real-time factor, or an equal-weighted mean, does as well for a fraction of the estimation cost. And the trailing-median normalisation that puts eight markets on one scale hides the level of volatility from every model; restoring it is worth more at long delays than the entire cross-section.

1. The question

A private equity portfolio is marked once a quarter. Direct property is appraised on a cycle, and the resulting series is smoothed in ways that have been documented for thirty years [8]. Hedge fund returns arrive with a serial correlation that understates their true volatility [7]. In each case someone must produce a risk number today from marks that are weeks or months old, while public markets have gone on trading in plain sight.

The instinct is to reach for those markets. The question is which of them, and how far it gets you. Two candidates present themselves. The first is breadth: other markets are correlated with yours, and some of them have already closed today. The second is the options market, which reports a forward-looking risk number continuously and without any delay at all. These are different purchases, and a practitioner choosing between a wider data feed and an options subscription would like to know which one buys back more of what staleness took.

We cannot observe the truth for a private portfolio, so we run the experiment where the truth is observable. We take a forecaster of S&P 500 realised variance, withhold their own data for δ days, leave the other sources untouched, and measure what each gives back. For foreign equities the asymmetry costs nothing to construct: Tokyo closes at 06:00 UTC, Hong Kong at 08:00, Mumbai at 10:00, London and Frankfurt at 16:30, New York at 21:00. On any given day the rest of the world has already finished trading when New York does.

2. What is already established

Four findings underpin this exercise, and none of them is ours.

Foreign equity volatility forecasts domestic volatility. Engle, Ito and Lin rejected the idea that volatility is a purely local phenomenon in 1990 [3], and the spillover literature since has measured the connections in considerable detail [2, 4]. Korkusuz augments a HAR model of S&P 500 volatility with a European block and improves on it [9]. Buncic and Gisler run the mirror experiment, forecasting seventeen foreign markets from US volatility, and report out-of-sample R² between 4.6% and 14.5% [1].

Non-synchronous closing times define which observations a forecaster may legitimately use. Zhao, Qiao and Lin say so explicitly and build an attention model whose mask prevents a closed exchange from acting as a source [15]. Jayawardena and co-authors forecast Tokyo volatility from markets that trade while Tokyo sleeps [6].

A live market can price a stale one. Goetzmann, Ivković and Rouwenhorst showed that same-day US returns predict the next day's international fund NAVs strongly enough to trade on [5]: the mechanism of this paper stripped to its simplest form.

Implied volatility carries much of the cross-market gain. This is the finding that shapes the design below. Both Korkusuz [9] and Buncic and Gisler [1] report that a large share of what looks like a cross-market effect is attributable to implied volatility, the VIX in particular. Their work does not settle the matter for a delayed forecaster, since in both cases the domestic information set is current, but it makes the comparison unavoidable: any claim that foreign markets substitute for timeliness has to be set against an options market that never goes stale.

What none of this work does is treat the delay as a variable. In every case additional data is added to an information set that is already current, and the question is whether it helps. Ours is the complementary question: when domestic information is not current, how much of the damage does each candidate undo, and how does that fraction move as the damage grows? That is a rate of substitution between things a practitioner can actually buy, and we could not find it measured.

3. How we measure it

The data are daily open, high, low and close for eight equity indices: S&P 500 (SPX), Nikkei 225 (N225), S&P/ASX 200 (AXJO), Hang Seng (HSI), Nifty 50 (NSEI), FTSE 100 (FTSE), DAX (DAX) and Bovespa (BVSP), from January 2000 to September 2026, together with three implied-volatility indices: CBOE VIX, its nine-day counterpart VIX9D, and VDAX-NEW. The two CBOE series are Cboe's own published daily histories; the eight indices and VDAX-NEW are retail end-of-day exports from Investing.com, downloaded in September 2026. The repository lists every file, the identifier it maps to, and the column dictionary, and pins the environment to NumPy and pandas alone. Each retail series came in two exports, because the source caps a download at 5,000 rows, which leaves nine joins to be checked rather than trusted. Eight of the nine overlap by 120 to 251 trading days, and across every overlapping row the two exports agree exactly: no mismatches, largest absolute difference zero. The ninth is the S&P itself, where the exports abut without overlapping and no cross-check is possible. We report it as unverified and fall back to two weaker tests: the join is contiguous, Friday 9 March 2018 to Monday 12 March 2018 with no session missing or duplicated, and the return across it is −0.0013, or 0.11 standard deviations of the series' own daily returns, so there is no level shift where the exports meet.

A foreign close is admissible only when its timestamp falls before the target's. Markets closing at or after the target therefore contribute their previous session. The rule generalises without special cases, which matters for Section 9: a Tokyo forecaster gets every other market a day late, because Tokyo closes first. It also disciplines the implied-volatility block, and the discipline is awkward in a way worth setting out precisely. VDAX-NEW satisfies the rule outright, since Frankfurt finishes hours before New York. VIX does not. Its printed daily close is stamped 21:15 UTC, fifteen minutes after the 21:00 S&P cash close, so that print is inadmissible. VIX is computed continuously from live SPX option quotes, so a value of it did exist at 21:00 and would be the right observation to use, but a retail end-of-day export carries no intraday snapshot and we do not have it. What we can run is a pair of timing specifications on either side of the one we want: the previous day's close, which is strictly admissible and discards a full day of information, and the same-day close, which contains fifteen minutes we are not entitled to. Section 8 leads with the lagged arm, because it is the one that obeys our own rule, and reports the same-day arm as a sensitivity check. The 21:00 observation is temporally between the two, and we are careful not to infer from that that its forecasting performance would be numerically between their results. Timestamps order; skill need not.

Volatility is the Yang-Zhang estimator [14] over rolling five-day windows, which uses the whole daily range and so sees more of each session than the close alone. Write Os, Hs, Ls and Cs for the open, high, low and close of session s, and

os = log(Os / Cs−1),   cs = log(Cs / Os),   us = log(Hs / Os),   ds = log(Ls / Os)

for the overnight, open-to-close, high and low log returns. Over the n sessions ending at t the estimator is

σ²YZ,t = σ²o + k σ²c + (1 − k) σ²RS,     k = 0.34 / (1.34 + (n+1)/(n−1)),(1)

where σ²o and σ²c are the sample variances of os and cs over those n sessions, σ²RS is the mean over them of the Rogers-Satchell term us(us − cs) + ds(ds − cs) [19], and n = 5 throughout. The three components measure the overnight gap, the drift within the session, and the range net of that drift; k is the weight for which the sum is minimum-variance and drift-independent under a Brownian price [14]. All variances use the n − 1 divisor.

Let i index the eight series and t the target's trading day. The decision variable each model sees is the log of that series' variance against its own recent level,

ai,t = log( σ²YZ,i,t / Mi,t ),     Mi,t = median( σ²YZ,i,s : t − 251 ≤ s ≤ t ),(2)

and the quantity to be forecast, with horizon h = 5 trading days and target market denoted SPX, is

yt = aSPX, t+h.(3)

So Mi,t is the trailing 252-day median, and the implied-volatility series enter the same way, as the log of the level over its own trailing median. Dividing by a series' own median puts Tokyo, São Paulo and the VIX on one scale and keeps each series' units out of the regression. One detail of that definition needs stating, because it looks like look-ahead and is not. The target at+5 carries Mt+5, a median whose window includes the five days between the forecast origin and the outcome, which a forecaster at t does not know. No feature is affected, since every one is dated t − δ or earlier for the domestic block and t for the others. What changes is that the quantity being predicted is not one a practitioner could convert back to a variance forecast at the moment they make it. The implementable alternative normalises the outcome at the ORIGIN, log(σ²YZ,t+5 / Mt), and we ran the whole experiment both ways. The two targets differ by the five-day drift in a 252-day median, which has a standard deviation 3.7% of the target's own and correlates +0.52 with it, so it is neither large nor independent. It changes nothing: the substitution rate is 71% against 72% at eleven weeks, and no delay differs by more than a point. We keep the target-dated definition and report the origin-dated run as the check that licenses it. The target is at+5, five trading days ahead, left continuous: a companion note [13] showed that splitting such a target at its median throws away exactly the magnitude information a cross-sectional model would use, so we do not split it.

Models are built from three feature blocks. The domestic block is the target's own history at daily, weekly and monthly aggregation, in the manner of Corsi [2], observed at t − δ. The foreign block is seven index decision variables at their admissible timestamps: the six that close before New York, plus Bovespa a session late. The implied-volatility block is VIX and VDAX. Only the domestic block is delayed; the others are what a forecaster could see today, and that asymmetry is the whole point of the comparison. Every model is a ridge regression refitted every 21 days on a rolling 1,250-day window, with the penalty chosen on a 250-day validation tail and features standardised on training statistics alone. Formally, let xt be the feature vector a model sees at t, assembled from the blocks above, and let T(t) be the 1,250 days of the training window in force at t. Features are centred and scaled by that window's own mean μT and standard deviation sT, giving zt = (xt − μT) / sT elementwise, and the coefficients (β0, β) solve

minβ0, β  ∑u ∈ T(t) ( yu − β0 − zu′β )²  +  λ β′β,(4)

with the intercept β0 left unpenalised and λ chosen from {0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30} on the last 250 days of the training window, which are held out of the fit that selects it. The forecast is ft = β0 + zt′β. Standardising on training statistics alone matters: scaling a feature by a full-sample standard deviation would let the test period influence the fit through the scale, which is a small leak but a real one. All are trained on the same labels, a pair being admitted only once its outcome was resolvable at t − δ, so differences between them are features and nothing else. The rolling fixed window is not incidental; it is what makes the tests in Sections 6 and 7 valid.

Losses need care here. With an imperfect volatility proxy only a restricted family of losses preserves the ranking a researcher would obtain against true latent volatility [17], and that family is defined on the VARIANCE scale. Our target is a log variance, so the two halves of the usual pairing land differently. QLIKE survives the transformation: it depends on the ratio of outcome to forecast alone, and dividing both by the same trailing median leaves that ratio untouched, so what we compute is exactly the variance-scale QLIKE. Squared error does not. Computed on log variance it is outside the family, because a monotone transform of the arguments is precisely what the robustness result fails to survive.

We therefore report three. Let m be the training-window mean of y, the benchmark forecast; let vt = exp(yt) Mt be realised variance on its natural scale and wt = exp(ft) Mt the corresponding variance forecast; and let n be the number of test days. The three losses are

R² = 1 − ∑t ( yt − ft )² ÷ ∑t ( yt − m )²,(5)

QLIKE = (1/n) ∑t [ vt/wt − log( vt/wt ) − 1 ],(6)

MSEvar = (1/n) ∑t ( vt − γt wt )²,     γt = (1 / |T(t)|) ∑u ∈ T(t) exp( yu − fu ),(7)

where γt is Duan's smearing factor [20], estimated from training-window residuals at each refit. Skill under QLIKE and MSEvar is defined as in (5), one minus the model's loss over the benchmark's, so all three columns are shares of a benchmark loss removed and are read the same way. Of the three, R² in (5) is the paper's headline and is not proxy-robust; QLIKE in (6) is; and MSEvar in (7) is Patton's MSE on the variance scale and is also robust. The smearing factor is there because exp() of a log forecast is median-unbiased, not mean-unbiased, and squared error on the variance scale punishes exactly that, so the reconstruction needs the correction, not the raw exponential. Mean absolute error, used in some recent work in this area, is in no version of the family. One choice inside that arrangement needs defending, because it looks backwards: the headline number in this paper is the one loss of the three that is NOT proxy-robust. We lead with log-scale R² because the paper's object is a ratio, R(δ), and a ratio needs a denominator a reader can hold: the share of a delay-induced loss recovered, naturally bounded near 0 and 100%, on the same scale as the companion note [13] and as the decision variable itself. QLIKE skill on a variance scale is the right loss and the wrong ruler for that purpose; its levels are not comparable to anything else in this literature. The defence is that the choice is presentational and is made where it can be checked: both proxy-robust losses are reported beside the headline in every table where a conclusion turns on one, and they support the same conclusions everywhere except the δ = 0 row, where they disagree with each other as well and Section 9 withdraws the claim instead of picking a winner. A reader who prefers the robust family throughout can read the QLIKE columns and will reach the same conclusions with different numbers attached. Where conclusions differ across losses we give weight to what both proxy-robust losses support; where those two disagree with each other, we treat the finding as unresolved, and we say so at the one place in the paper where that happens.

Write S(δ) for skill at delay δ under any one of (5), (6) or (7): the share of the benchmark's loss that a model removes. Sown(δ) is the skill of the model holding only the domestic block, δ days stale; Salt(δ) is the skill of the same model with a candidate substitute added. Delay costs Sown(0) − Sown(δ); the substitute returns Salt(δ) − Sown(δ). The substitution rate is their ratio,

R(δ) = [ Salt(δ) − Sown(δ) ] ÷ [ Sown(0) − Sown(δ) ].(8)

It has no units, so it can be compared across loss functions, across markets, and across candidates. One caution, which Section 8 will need: the measure assumes the substitute is restoring something delay destroyed. A source that also carries information the domestic history never had can exceed 100%, and when it does, the ratio has stopped answering the question it was built for. The denominator can fail in the other direction too: where delay costs a forecaster little, Sown(0) − Sown(δ) approaches zero and the ratio need not have a bounded confidence set. The Appendix gives a construction that stays honest there, and Section 10.2 applies it to the state where the risk is greatest. On this sample the denominator never gets close enough for the pathology to bite, but it is a property of the measure itself, and anyone reusing R(δ) on a series where delay does little damage should check it rather than assume it.

One warning attaches to every figure in that third column before the paper goes further, and Section 10.2 establishes it. An unconditional rate averages two states in which this mechanism behaves quite differently. In stressed markets it is 81% at eleven weeks with an interval of [74, 87], and it is 81% in each half of the sample taken separately: a well-determined, stable number. In calm markets the same quantity spans ninety points and cannot be pinned down at all, for a mechanical reason: where delay costs a forecaster almost nothing, the share of that cost repaired by anything has no stable value. Table 1's 72% is the average of a result and a non-result. We report it because it is the prespecified estimate and because the literature quotes unconditional rates, but the number a practitioner should carry is the conditional one in Table 17.

Three things stand out. At one day the rate is indistinguishable from zero, as it should be: the S&P's own recent history is still the best available description of the S&P, and the foreign block we selected produces no measurable net improvement on it. By eleven weeks the picture has inverted. The domestic model's out-of-sample R² has gone negative, meaning it would be better to forecast the training mean, while the cross-sectional model still holds 0.347, about 70% of what was available with fully current data. And between those extremes the rate climbs steeply and then slows, which raises the obvious question of where it stops.

Section 3 promised a third loss, and it belongs here because it is the robust one that most resembles the headline. Under squared error on the variance scale the substitution rate reads 85% at five days and 90% at eleven weeks, against 51% and 72% on the log scale. Both robust losses therefore put the rate ABOVE the headline, which makes the number we lead with the conservative one. The variance-scale intervals are very wide at short delays, [−74%, 126%] at five days, because the denominator there is small and squared error on raw variance is dominated by a handful of crisis days; that is why QLIKE, not MSE, is the robust loss we lean on.

5. Why breadth stops where it does

The rate flattens near three-quarters instead of approaching one, and the reason is not subtle once measured. At very large delay the domestic model retains little usable out-of-sample skill, so the cross-sectional model is working almost entirely from foreign information. Its ceiling is therefore whatever share of the S&P's volatility the rest of the world can see.

We measured that share directly. Regressing the S&P's decision variable on the six foreign closes of the same day gives R² = 0.695. Roughly 30% of the S&P's current volatility state is not linearly spanned by those six series in this sample. We resist calling that share genuinely local, because the regression does not show it: a non-linear function of the same closes, or a wider cross-section, could reach some of it. What the number does bound is how much a LINEAR model of THESE markets can see, the ceiling the fitted model is working against. Predicting five days ahead from foreign closes alone gives R² = 0.399 against 0.498 from domestic history, or 80% of it, which brackets the 72% the fitted model achieves. The gap between the in-sample bound and the fitted figure is consistent with the price of estimating parameters instead of knowing them, and Section 5 measures a cost of the right order; the design does not establish that estimation is the whole of it.

The cross-section is also nearly one-dimensional. The first principal component of the six foreign volatility series accounts for 66.5% of their variation and the first two for 76.9%. Six markets are close to one global volatility factor plus idiosyncratic noise, which carries a practical implication: an eighth or a ninth correlated index may add relatively little under this linear specification, because what a linear model of these markets can see is set by how much of the S&P is global; counting more global series does not move it.

That leaves the other half of the sentence above: what it costs to estimate parameters instead of knowing them. We had treated this as a residual, and it is large enough to deserve a number. Breadth is not free. Seven foreign coefficients must be estimated from a finite window, and the estimation is paid for whether or not the coefficients turn out to matter.

Two measurements agree on the size of the bill. The first varies the number of peers at δ = 0 and reads off the slope: the penalty is a straight line through the origin at −0.0014 of R² per market, which accounts for 98% of the variation across peer counts and implies −0.0100 for the full seven. The second replaces the foreign block with AR(1) surrogates fitted to each peer, drawing innovations from a multivariate normal with the peers' own contemporaneous covariance so the surrogate cross-section is as correlated as the real one, while carrying no information about the target at any lag. Averaged over one hundred draws this gives −0.0131 at δ = 0, with a Monte Carlo standard error of 0.0003, an order of magnitude below the sampling interval that follows and therefore not the uncertainty that binds. The two methods differ by 0.003, which agrees on the magnitude without being a formal match, and we do not read a third decimal place from either. About one point of R² is the price of carrying the cross-section. Section 5.2 measures it a third time, with a generator that fits nothing at all, and gets the same answer.

Two things follow. The first settles a reading we had left open. At δ = 0 the measured difference is −0.0103 and the cost is −0.0131, so the gross content of the foreign block is +0.0028. Gross is a difference of two estimated quantities, so we bootstrap it, resampling test days in moving blocks and recomputing the measured difference and the surrogate cost on the same days so that their errors stay correlated. The interval is [−0.0060, +0.0115]: indistinguishable from zero, and bounded above at about one point of R². Foreign markets are not misleading about the current state of the S&P. Whatever they know about it that fully current domestic data does not already carry is too small for this design to detect, and the negative sign was the bill, not the information.

The second is that the bill grows with delay, from −0.013 at δ = 0 to −0.054 at δ = 21. This is a property of the fitting procedure: as domestic information goes stale the fit weakens, the validation step selects a looser penalty, and the seven regressors are given more rope. Separating the two makes the gross and net readings diverge in a way that matters for anyone deciding whether to build this.

5.1 Fitting the cheaper representation, instead of inferring it

Two facts above sit next to each other without being joined. The foreign block is nearly one-dimensional, its first principal component accounting for 66.5% of the variation and its first two for 76.9%. Carrying seven regressors costs about a point of R² at δ = 0 and more as the delay grows, measured by replacing the block with surrogates that carry no signal. It is natural to conclude that a cheaper parameterisation would recover part of that cost. That is an inference from a hypothetical generator, not a demonstration, and the demonstration is cheap.

So compress the block and compare. The eigenvectors are recomputed at every refit from the covariance of the training window alone, on columns standardised with training statistics alone, with each eigenvector's sign pinned so the factor does not flip arbitrarily between refits. A basis computed once on the whole sample would leak: “the first component of the foreign block” is not something a forecaster in 2009 could have written down from 2026 data. An equal-weighted mean of the seven series is included as the compression that requires no estimation at all.

One real-time factor beats all seven series at every delay, by 0.012 to 0.019 of R², and the margin grows with delay exactly as the surrogate cost does. The gain reaches conventional significance at δ = 0 and δ = 55 and falls short of it in between, so the pattern is more convincing than any single entry. The real-time first component explains 66.6% of the block's variance averaged over refits, against 66.5% in the full-sample figure quoted above, which says the basis is stable enough to be estimated as you go.

Reporting a new preferred specification on the log scale alone would be inconsistent with Section 7, which exists to establish that the scale changes the answer. Under QLIKE, the proxy-robust loss, one factor wins at every delay, exactly as it does under R². Under squared error on the variance scale a compression wins at five of six delays; the exception is δ = 0, where the seven-regressor model scores lowest. That loss is dominated by a handful of crisis days, so Section 7 reports its intervals as uninformative at short delays, so the exception is a statement about tails. Where the three losses appear to disagree elsewhere they are choosing between one factor and two, which differ by thousandths and are not a disagreement about anything.

The compression also raises the substitution rate, and a referee has asked which number the paper is claiming. Recomputed with the same full-ratio bootstrap as the headline, R(55) is 71.6% [63.6%, 78.8%] for the seven-regressor model, 74.3% [67.2%, 80.9%] for the equal-weighted mean and 75.2% [68.2%, 81.8%] for one factor. Every compressed estimate sits inside the interval the seven-regressor model already carries, so the two are not distinguishable and we do not replace the headline with the larger figure. We report 72% as the prespecified full-block estimate, note the compressed estimate beside it, and say plainly that the difference is inside the noise of both.

Two qualifications, and the first is the more important. The equal-weighted mean captures nearly all of the gain: PC1 beats it, but by 0.001 to 0.005. What is being bought is the decision to compress, not the machinery of compressing, and a practitioner should take the average and skip the eigenvector. Second, this changes the δ = 0 row. The seven-coefficient model scores 0.4884 against the domestic model's 0.4987, the small negative this paper has declined to interpret, while PC1 scores 0.5001, above it. We do not claim the cross-section helps at δ = 0 on that basis: it is a difference of 0.0014 with no interval attached, and reading an uninterval-ed difference as a finding is the specific error this paper has made and corrected three times. What the row supports is narrower and sufficient, that the negative at δ = 0 belongs to the parameterisation and not to the information.

5.2 Is the bill an artefact of the generator?

Both measurements of the bill are relative to something. The slope method extrapolates from peer counts; the surrogate method replaces the foreign block with fitted Gaussian AR(1) series. Under that objection the cost is identified only relative to a generator we chose, and a forecaster's real regressors are not Gaussian AR(1) series.

The objection has a direct test. Measure the bill again with a generator that fits nothing at all: take the real foreign block and move it circularly through the sample. Every marginal, every tail, every cluster is then the real one, and the only thing destroyed is the alignment with the target. If an unfitted generator returns the fitted generator's number, the objection is answered, not conceded.

5.3 Is breadth a global factor by another name?

A referee raises omitted-variable bias: foreign breadth may not be capturing cross-border diffusion at all, but proxying for a global systemic risk factor, so that what looks like transmission is simultaneous reaction to shared news. Section 5.1 is most of the way to agreeing, since one real-time factor beats all seven series at every delay, but it never separates the two halves and asks which one carries the recovery. The decomposition is available at no cost, because the basis is already being estimated.

At each refit, write v1 for the leading eigenvector of the training covariance of the standardised foreign block. Let pt be that block's seven decision variables at t, standardised on the training window, and let v1 be the unit eigenvector belonging to the largest eigenvalue of their training covariance, with its sign pinned so that the first loading is positive. The split is

pt = gt v1 + rt,     gt = pt′v1,     rt = pt − gt v1,(9)

where gt is the scalar global factor and rt is the six-dimensional remainder orthogonal to v1. Sign pinning is not cosmetic: an eigenvector is defined only up to sign, and one that flipped between refits would enter the regression as noise unrelated to the data. The + PC1 column below uses gt as a single regressor and + remainder uses the six components of rt. Each day's standardised row x splits into a global part (x·v1)v1 and a remainder orthogonal to it, spanned by components two through seven. Adding the two back together is a rotation of the raw block and must reproduce it: it does, to within 0.0010 of R² at every delay, the check that licenses reading the split.

6. A model of the delay curve

Everything above is a measurement. Two of those measurements are close to a specification, and writing it down turns the curve in Figure 1 from a description into a prediction with a testable ceiling.

Section 5.3 found the recovery entirely in the leading component of the foreign block, with the six orthogonal components worth less than nothing. Section 10.1 finds that the level of volatility, which equation (2) removes, carries information that grows with delay. Together they say: log variance is a slow level, plus a factor several markets share, plus something idiosyncratic. For market i,

log σ²i,t = μi,t + λi gt + ei,t,     gt = φ gt−1 + ut,(10)

with μ the slow level the 252-day median tracks, g a persistent global factor, e idiosyncratic and u white noise. The decision variable of equation (2) subtracts an estimate of μ, so ai,t ≈ λi gt + ei,t. That is the whole of Section 10.1 in one line: the normalisation removes μ by construction, so restoring it is worth something.

A forecaster δ days late sees aSPX,t−δ, one noisy reading of gt−δ. The cross-section supplies seven readings of gt, whose common component suppresses the idiosyncratic part. Under (10),

E[ gt+h | gt−δ ] = φ h+δ gt−δ,     E[ gt+h | gt ] = φ h gt,(11)

and three consequences follow, each checkable against quantities measured elsewhere in this paper, not fitted to the curve.

First, the stale forecaster's skill decays geometrically: log Sown(δ) is linear in δ with slope 2 log φ. Fitted over the eight delays where Sown is positive, the line has R² = 0.9984 and slope −0.07217 per day, implying φ = 0.9646. The factor's own persistence, estimated from the principal-component series and never shown the decay curve, is 0.9678. The two agree to 0.0033. The delay curve is the factor's memory, seen through a forecast.

Second, Scross(δ) should be near-flat, because the foreign block delivers gt whatever the domestic delay is. Across eleven weeks it falls 29%, against 107% for the domestic model. Perfect flatness is not predicted, because the domestic block is still in the cross-sectional model and still decays, but the contrast is the model's.

Third, and most useful: R(δ) rises from zero towards a ceiling below one, and the model names it. Since Sown(δ) → 0,

R(∞) = Scross ÷ Sown(0),(12)

which is below one because seven peers estimate gt with error and never observe eSPX,t. Both quantities are measured elsewhere: Scross(55) = 0.3470 and Sown(0) = 0.4987, giving a predicted ceiling of 69.6%. The measured curve reaches 69.8% at δ = 34 and 71.6% at δ = 55. Nothing in that prediction was fitted to the curve it predicts.

So the substitution rate is not an arbitrary rising function. It is a factor share, it converges, and the paper can say what it converges to. The model also explains why Section 5.3's orthogonal remainder is worthless, being e, which forecasts nothing, and why Section 9.3's staleness penalty exists, since an aged foreign block reads g at t − g, not at t and (11) prices that at φ per day. Three results the paper had separately are one mechanism.

What the model does not explain is why the mechanism is stronger in stress. Equation (11) holds λ and φ constant; Section 10.2 shows the factor doing more work when markets are frightened, which would need λ or the variance of u to move with state. We have not fitted that, and it is the obvious next specification.

7. Does it survive testing

Bootstrap intervals describe a difference; they do not test a hypothesis, and our domestic model is nested inside our cross-sectional one, which rules out the usual comparison. Diebold has written at length about the misuse of his own test in exactly this situation [12]. We use Giacomini-White [10] as the primary test, because it is valid for nested models and, being loss-agnostic, covers the QLIKE result as well as the squared-error one. It requires a fixed rolling estimation window, of the kind Section 3 describes. Writing Ltown and Ltalt for the two models' losses on test day t and Dt = Ltown − Ltalt for the loss differential, the statistic is

GW = d ÷ SE(d),     SE(d)² = (1/n) [ γ0 + 2 ∑j=1L (1 − j/(L+1)) γj ],(13)

where d is the mean of Dt over the n test days, γj is its sample autocovariance at lag j, and the Bartlett weights (1 − j/(L+1)) run to L = 9 lags. The HAC correction is not optional here: a five-day-ahead target overlaps on four of every five days, so the raw standard error of a daily loss differential understates by roughly a factor of two. Positive GW favours the model with the substitute, and we report two-sided p-values from the standard normal throughout. Clark-West [11] serves as a second opinion on squared loss, with a qualification developed in Section 7.1 that is the reason it is the second opinion, not the first.

The primary test is untouched, and for a reason, not by luck. The argument above turns on Clark-West being a test about models, so that anything changing the estimation penalty changes what its adjustment ought to be. Giacomini-White is a test about methods under a fixed rolling window, and a method is the whole procedure including whatever tuning happens inside that window. The validation slice is carved out of the training window, so it is part of what is being tested rather than a contaminant of a correction, and Giacomini-White makes no finite-sample correction that could be thrown off. The prediction is that its column barely moves under the remedy, and the last two columns of Table 7 are that prediction tested: no statistic moves by more than 0.06 and no verdict changes at any delay. The substitution rates, the bootstrap intervals and the simultaneous bands involve no nested-model correction at all and are unaffected.

A second concern is that we test ten delays at once. Ten pointwise 95% intervals will breach somewhere by chance, so we bootstrapped the whole vector jointly, in moving blocks of ten days to respect the overlap in the target [18], and took the largest studentised deviation across the grid. The resulting critical value is 6.51 instead of 1.96, a heavy penalty, and the effect still clears it from δ = 3 to δ = 55. One casualty is worth naming: the small negative reading at δ = 0 does not survive the simultaneous band. We do not claim the cross-section is harmful when domestic data is current, and Section 5 gives the reason it is not: net of what it costs to estimate, its content there is zero.

The third concern is that the result is an artefact of fitting a wide model to a persistent series. We tested it by shifting the entire foreign block five years through the sample. The series keep their distributions, their autocorrelation, their crises; they lose only their alignment with the target. The substitution rate falls from +72% to −8%, and is negative at every delay we checked. Whatever the model is using, it is the alignment. The residual negative is not noise around zero and should not be read as one: it is the cost of breadth from Section 5, which is all that remains once the information is removed. A placebo of this kind lands below zero by construction, and a version of it that came back at exactly zero would be the surprising result.

Finally, Section 4 described the rate as slowing, which is a claim, not an observation. Comparing R(55) with earlier values: against R(13) the difference is +0.079 with an interval of [+0.037, +0.129], and against R(21) it is +0.042 with [+0.014, +0.076], both still rising. Against R(34) it is +0.018 with [−0.001, +0.039], which does not reject equality. The rate is still climbing out to about seven weeks and flat thereafter.

One last property of the rate was assumed and never tested. The rate is a ratio whose denominator is what the delay costs a domestic-only forecaster, and at short delays that quantity is near zero. A ratio with a denominator that can vanish is weakly identified, and a percentile bootstrap of it reports finite endpoints whether or not they mean anything. We therefore recomputed every interval by inverting a HAC t-test after Fieller, which returns two half-lines or the whole line when the data do not pin the ratio down. At every delay from one day to eleven weeks the confidence set is an ordinary interval, and Fieller's endpoints sit within 2.4 percentage points of the bootstrap's throughout: at δ = 55, [63%, 79%] against [64%, 78%]. The objection is right in principle and inert here, so the published intervals stand. The Appendix gives the construction, the one delay where the ratio does not exist at all, and the error that revealed the first version to be wrong.

That should not be mysterious, though it should not be overstated either. The VIX is a RISK-NEUTRAL expectation of S&P variance over roughly the coming MONTH; our target is REALISED variance over the coming five days. The two differ in horizon, by a factor of about four, and in measure, by a variance risk premium that is itself time-varying. So the VIX is not the quantity we are predicting. It is, however, a purpose-built forward-looking forecast of something close to it, and comparing that with a cross-section of correlated state variables is comparing a forecast with a set of clues. Read that way the surprise is not that implied volatility wins but that seven foreign equity markets get within two-thirds of it despite the horizon and measure mismatch working against the options market.

The sixth column is what the objection actually turns on, and it is negative at every delay. Given implied volatility, adding the foreign block makes the forecast slightly worse, and the Giacomini-White statistic, for which the domestic-plus-IV model is nested inside the full one so the same test applies as in Section 7, reaches conventional significance at the short delays. Taken literally this says foreign closes damage a forecast that already has VIX, which would be strange behaviour for genuine information.

Section 5 supplies the alternative reading, and the same instrument settles it. Replacing the seven foreign series with AR(1) surrogates matched on persistence and variance leaves regressors that carry nothing by construction, and what remains is the bill for estimating them. It comes to roughly 0.011 to 0.015 of R², the whole of the negative. The content of the foreign block given implied volatility, gross of that cost, is +0.001 at δ = 0, +0.007 at δ = 5, +0.008 at δ = 21 and +0.007 at δ = 55, and bootstrapping each of those the way Section 5 describes gives intervals that all cover zero: [−0.007, +0.010], [−0.002, +0.017], [−0.002, +0.019] and [−0.003, +0.017]. The correct word is redundant. Foreign closes do not damage a forecast that already sees the options market; they add nothing to it, and paying to estimate nothing is what makes the measured figure negative.

The table above is the admissible specification: VIX lagged a full day, which discards a day of information the live index would have carried. Substituting the same-day close, which contains fifteen minutes we are not entitled to, moves the options market the other way: R² at δ = 21 rises from 0.507 to 0.568, a substitution rate of 119%, while the incremental contribution of the foreign block stays negative at all ten delays and its gross content stays indistinguishable from zero. Neither arm is the specification we would choose, and the one we would choose is not available to us. What licenses a conclusion here is not that the 21:00 observation falls between these two in time, which would say nothing about how it forecasts, but that the verdict is the same under a specification that gives the options market a full day less information and under one that gives it fifteen minutes more. A finding that survives that much variation in the input is not resting on the timing choice.

One feature of that result deserves a sentence, because it is more striking than it first looks. VIX is a thirty-calendar-day implied volatility and the target here is realised variance over five trading days, so the winning instrument is measured over a horizon six times longer than the thing it is forecasting. That it dominates anyway says something about how much of the five-day question is answered by the level of the whole term structure, not by its front. The matched instrument exists, since Cboe publishes a nine-day index, VIX9D, formerly VXST, and Section 8.5 runs the race again with it. The horizon does matter, by rather more than we expected, and the conclusion of this section does not.

8.3 Is five days the result, or the setting?

Every number above forecasts five trading days ahead, and a reader is entitled to ask whether that is a finding or a convention. So the whole experiment is re-run at one day, two weeks and one month. One detail has to be settled before the table can be read: the Yang-Zhang proxy spans five days, which is how the latent variance is measured and has nothing to do with how far ahead we forecast, but it means that at h = 1 the outcome window shares four of its five days with the domestic feature the model already holds. Nothing leaks, since the outcome is still strictly in the future and every feature is still dated t − δ or earlier for the domestic block. But a forecaster at one day is being asked to predict an object they can largely already see, and their R² at δ = 0 duly comes out at 0.921 against 0.499 at five days. The serial correlation of the target confirms it: 0.953 at one day against 0.692 at five, 0.576 at ten and 0.417 at twenty-one.

8.4 What the R² column means to someone sizing a position

A point of R² on log variance is not an intuitive quantity, and the reading a risk manager needs is narrower than the column suggests. What breadth repairs is a stale forecaster's tendency to over-estimate variance; the understating tail is unmoved: it runs between 8.6% and 11.4% of days whatever the delay and whichever model is used. Concretely, at eleven weeks the stale domestic forecaster's variance forecast is off by a median factor of 1.90, and is more than a factor of two out on 59.2% of days against 37.1% for the forecaster holding the cross-section, against 31.1% for a forecaster with no delay at all. Breadth closes most of that gap, and all of what it closes is over-estimation. Anyone reaching for the cross-section as protection against being surprised by a volatility spike is reaching for the wrong instrument.

9. Where each result holds

The same exercise with each of five indices as the target is in Table 19, on the continuous target and at every delay.

The effect appears everywhere, and the Nikkei is the case that carries information. It closes before every other market in the sample, so it has no same-day foreign observation at all, since every peer reaches it a session late, and it recovers the least, 60% against 69% to 88% elsewhere. That is what the mechanism predicts and what a spurious correlation would not produce. Past that contrast we can say little: the S&P has six same-day peers and recovers less than the FTSE with four, the intervals overlap heavily, and five markets cannot resolve an ordering.

One further objection deserves an answer. Rapach, Strauss and Zhou find that lagged US returns predict foreign returns while the reverse is weak [16]. That concerns returns while ours concerns volatility, and lagged returns where ours are same-day closes observed before the target's own close. We rely on both distinctions, and a reader who rejects them should discount Sections 4 through 6 accordingly.

The scope condition matters more than the ordering. Section 8 shows that where a liquid options market exists on the asset, breadth is redundant, and the S&P 500 is the most heavily optioned index in the world. So the breadth result may be most relevant to assets without such a market, the case Section 1 opens with: a private portfolio marked quarterly, a property book on an appraisal cycle, a fund reporting NAV with a lag. Nobody writes a liquid option on those. What a holder of them can see is the public cross-section, and Sections 4 through 6 say what that is worth as a function of how stale the mark has become.

We would rather state that plainly than dress it up, because it is also the weakest point of the design. We ran the experiment on a liquid asset in order to observe the truth, and the property that makes the truth observable, a deep and continuously traded market, is the same property that brings an options market with it. The result we can measure cleanly and the case we care about are therefore separated by the one feature that decides which substitute wins. Section 11 says what would close that gap.

There remains the δ = 0 row of Table 1, and it is the one place in this paper where the choice of loss decides the sign. On log-scale squared error the cross-section is worse by 0.0103, interval [−0.0192, −0.0016]; on QLIKE it is worse by 0.0142, [−0.0292, −0.0023]; on squared error on the variance scale it is BETTER by 0.0071, [−0.0470, +0.0280]. Two of the three exclude zero and point one way, the third does not exclude zero and points the other. An earlier draft read the negative as settled and used it to press a disagreement with Korkusuz [9] and Jayawardena et al. [6], who find foreign volatility improves a current forecast. We withdraw that. A sign that moves with a monotone transformation of the target is not a finding about the world, and the two losses that agree are the log-scale one, which is not proxy-robust, and QLIKE, which shares the log-ratio structure. The honest statement is that at zero delay our design cannot tell which way the effect runs, and nothing here bears on their result. Table 2's decomposition still holds conditional on the log scale: whatever is there is small, bounded near one point of R², and mostly the bill for estimating seven coefficients.

9.1 In stress and in calm

Everything above is an average over 4,862 days, and a referee objects that the interesting days are not average: a rolling median assumes a stationary volatility distribution, and across a structural macro transition it should fail to adjust. The objection is testable here because the test window contains the 2008 crisis, 2011, August 2015, February 2018, COVID and the 2022 inflation shock. It is not testable on the companion note, whose window begins in November 2023 and contains no transition at all, and the note says so instead of answering it.

Two decisions shape what follows. The first is that a single split is not enough. Six named episodes at full width run to 866 days, most of them ordinary: "the 2022 inflation shock" as the whole of 2022 is 251 days. A wide window dilutes exactly what is being tested. So the definition is tightened in rungs: VIX at t−1 above its own trailing 67th, 90th and 95th percentile, which needs no dates and is available to a forecaster in real time, together with the named episodes at full width and as the 41 trading days centred on each window's VIX peak. The tightening was added after the wide version had been run, and we say so instead of presenting it as the original design.

The second decision matters more. Squared errors are larger when the target is more volatile, so a larger loss differential in stress can be nothing but heteroskedasticity. Every figure below is therefore the differential divided by the domestic model's own mean squared error inside the same regime: a share of error removed, comparable across states in a way the raw difference is not. The raw column is reported beside it, and the two tell different stories, and that is the reason for the precaution.

9.2 Does implied volatility stop dominating when the options market is stressed?

The mirror objection to Section 8 is that redundancy given VIX and VDAX assumes a well-functioning options surface, and that during a crunch breadth might stop being redundant exactly when it matters. Applying the same ladder to the increment the foreign block adds on top of the implied-volatility model, across four delays and six states, the foreign block adds significantly in none of twenty-four cells, on the stressed rungs and the calm ones alike, down to the top 5% of VIX days. Redundancy given implied volatility is not an average concealing a stressed subsample. The severest rung carries 324 days, so an effect confined to conditions more extreme than the ladder reaches would not appear here.

One reading of that decay has to be ruled out before the rest is safe. If the foreign block worked by propagation, with volatility travelling from Tokyo to New York overnight, its value would be its earliness, the decay above would be the wave passing, and the right response would be a richer dynamic model, not a warning about feeds. The cross-correlations appear to speak to this and they are a trap. Correlating each peer at t−1 against the target at t, then the target at t−1 against the peer at t, all seven peers appear to LAG the target. But a row of this panel is not an instant. The target closes at 21:00 UTC and a peer closes c hours earlier, so the first direction spans 24 + c hours and the second 24 − c; for a persistent series the shorter span gives the higher correlation, and the clock alone predicts the observed sign whether or not anything leads anything.

That prediction is quantitative, so it can be tested instead of conceded. Each peer's asymmetry should scale with 2c. Across the seven it does, with a correlation of +0.93: 0.013 and 0.019 for the FTSE and the DAX, four and a half hours ahead of New York, against 0.051 for Bovespa, a full day ahead once admissibility has shifted it. The apparent lead–lag structure is a property of the calendar, not of the markets, and it is evidence for neither story. A check from the other side agrees: replacing the seven peers with the single real-time global factor of Section 5.1 and entering it at one lag at a time, the contemporaneous factor beats every lagged version in 26 of 30 cells. Yesterday's factor does add to today's at δ = 3 and δ = 5, and at no longer delay, so a little genuine dynamics is present, inside an otherwise contemporaneous story.

What is left is the reading Section 5.3 already arrived at by a different route. The block is a second reading of one shared state, it loses value as it ages at about the rate the first reading does, and it stops contributing once it is older than the mark it was brought in to replace: at δ = 13 a foreign block ten days stale returns the model to an effective age of 13.2 days, the domestic mark on its own. Breadth substitutes for a stale mark by being fresh, not by being early.

10. What the rate depends on

R(δ) carries Sown in three of its four places, so every figure above is a statement about the domestic model as much as about the foreign one. That is uncomfortable, because the paper has spent its effort on the other side: Section 5.1 compresses the foreign block, Section 5.3 splits it, Section 8.1 tries it as one regressor. The control has been the same three features throughout. That is a finding about this paper's own design, not about volatility. Equation (2) divides every series by its own trailing median, and that is what puts Tokyo, São Paulo and the VIX on one scale and makes a cross-section possible at all. It is also what prevents every model in the paper from seeing how volatile the world actually is. A forecaster who has lost his recent observations still knows roughly what regime he was in, and that knowledge is worth more the more he has lost, which is where the paper claims its largest effect. The comparability the design buys is paid for in domestic information, the price rises with delay, and we have not found this trade priced anywhere in the literature the paper cites. Anyone normalising a panel this way should measure what it costs before assuming it is free.

That claim has an obvious objection and it has to be answered rather than noted. The target is log(σ² / Mt+5), M is a 252-day median and therefore very slow, so a model handed log Mt−δ could lower its squared error simply by predicting the target's own DENOMINATOR. That would be arithmetic about how the target was built, not a forecast of volatility. Nothing would be inadmissible, since every feature is dated t − δ, but the finding would be empty.

The test is to re-date the normaliser and see what survives. Three targets were built, differing in nothing else: the paper's, normalised at t+5; the origin-dated one of Section 3, normalised at t; and one normalised at t − δ, the forecaster's own information set. The third is decisive, because its denominator is known exactly when the forecast is made, so a model cannot buy anything by predicting a quantity it has already been given, and it is also the only one of the three a delayed forecaster could actually use. Across the four positive delays the third target retains between 87% and 102% of the gain: +0.0680 against +0.0667 at eleven weeks. Re-dating the normaliser changes nothing, so the level is not being used to undo the normalisation. It is information.

Two qualifications. The gain reaches conventional significance only at δ = 55, on all three targets alike, so the short-delay column shows a direction without establishing a result. And the rate correction above survives the same test: on the implementable target the level moves R(55) from 74% to 70%, a shift of the same size as the 72% to 68% reported here.

10.2 Is the rate stable over nineteen years?

Section 9.1 conditions on market state; this asks the different question of whether the result drifts in time. Split at the midpoint, the two halves agree at every delay, 77% against 67% at eleven weeks, with overlapping intervals at all four. An unconditional rate averages a well-measured result with an unmeasurable one, and Section 4 says so instead of quoting the average alone.

Conditioning on the VIX raises a fair objection, since Section 8 says that a forecaster who holds the VIX should ignore the cross-section altogether. The two are compatible, but only for a specific reader, and it is worth being exact about who. Such a reader can therefore condition on market state without being able to buy the substitute that state variable makes redundant. A reader who holds options on their own asset is in Section 8's case and should stop reading at Section 8. We also note that Section 9.1 works with a ladder of cuts at the 33rd, 90th and 95th percentiles while this section uses terciles, and that the tercile split was chosen after that ladder had been seen.

10.3 How many models were tried

Thirty-three scripts stand behind this paper and nine distinct forecasters have been fitted to this target across them. A single test statistic on the one that won is not the right instrument for that, so the whole set is put through White's Reality Check and Hansen's SPA, with the size of both first validated by simulation on this sample's own dependence structure, where a nominal 5% test rejects 5.5% and 7.0% of the time respectively, with SPA rejecting more often, as Hansen predicts. Against the domestic benchmark, the best of the nine survives both tests at δ = 3, 5, 13, 21 and 55 with p below 0.0005. It fails at δ = 0, where the best margin across all nine models is +0.0014 of R²: nothing beats the benchmark there at all, so that failure is an absence of any effect to correct.

10.4 Leakage from an overlapping target

The target is realised variance over the next five days, so consecutive observations share four days of outcome, and a naive split would put part of the answer in the training set. Every refit here already cuts the training window at t − δ − h, which removes exactly the rows whose outcome was unresolved at the forecast date: a purge, though the paper has not called it one. Removing it inflates R² by +0.0024 [+0.00062, +0.00387] at zero delay and +0.0021 [+0.00017, +0.00382] at three days, so it is load-bearing and any study of a five-day target that splits without one is reporting inflated numbers. Adding an embargo on top of it costs nothing detectable in any of eighteen cells, so the gap the design already leaves is sufficient.

11. What we cannot say

The limitation that matters most is the one Section 9 names. The illiquid-asset case that motivates the whole exercise is argued rather than demonstrated, and Section 8 raises the cost of that gap rather than leaving it where it was: we now know the answer depends on whether an options market exists on the asset, and we have tested only the side where one does. Section 13 narrows the gap by one step, showing that the clean-lag model of staleness behaves like periodic marking at matched mean staleness, so the apparatus is at least the right shape for the case we cannot reach. It does not close it. Only a genuine appraisal-based or quarterly-marked target, on an asset with no options written on it, would do that.

Section 9.3 adds a limitation that applies to every number in this paper and to no single section of it. All of them were computed with the foreign block at its freshest admissible value, and the paper did not say so because it did not occur to us to vary it. A desk whose own foreign feed is one day behind recovers 29% of the delay-induced loss at five days where Table 1 reports 51%, and one three days behind recovers none of it. The measurement is not wrong, but the claim is narrower than it reads: it describes a forecaster with a stale domestic mark and a current foreign feed, a more particular creature than the one Section 1 has in mind. Whether an illiquid-asset holder is that creature is a question about their data vendor, not about volatility, and we cannot answer it.

One limitation from Section 10 qualifies the headline itself: the unconditional rate averages two states in which the mechanism behaves differently. In stressed markets it is well determined, stable across both halves of the sample, and large in absolute terms as well as in share. In calm markets it is imprecise enough to be uninformative. Table 1 is an average of the two, and the conditional figures in Table 17 are what a reader should carry.

The implied-volatility block is thinner than we would like. VIX and VDAX cover the sample. A spot VSTOXX index was obtained and tested, and it cannot join the headline block because requiring it costs 73% of the sample: insisting all three series be present moves the window from 4,525 days beginning September 2008 to 1,240 days beginning April 2020, which discards the 2008 crisis outright and begins after the March 2020 spike: peak VIX is 82.7 over the full sample and 41.4 inside what remains. Holding those 1,240 days fixed and varying only the block, the third series adds nothing at any delay and is mildly negative at all six, because VSTOXX and VDAX are both European equity volatility indices;4 reports what it shows; the short version is that it does not cover the sample either, so the headline block stays at two series. On the window where three series are available, the third one makes the options arm slightly worse, because VSTOXX and VDAX are both European volatility indices and the third column costs more to estimate than it returns. The conclusion it was defending, that the foreign block is redundant given implied volatility, is unaffected, so the error was worth correcting rather than quietly dropping.

The non-linearity result in Section 8.2 is bounded in a specific way. A random Fourier layer is a one-hidden-layer network whose first layer is drawn rather than learned, and no network with a learned first layer was fitted. The argument that one would not change the answer is an extension of the estimation-cost finding: more free parameters, the same bill, a sample that has repeatedly failed to pay it, and a capacity comparison already pointing the wrong way. An extension is not a test. Section 8.4 names a limitation of a different kind, and it is the one a practitioner should read first: what breadth repairs is a stale forecaster's tendency to over-estimate variance, not their exposure to under-estimating it. The understating tail is unmoved at every delay we checked. Anyone reaching for the cross-section as protection against being caught short by a volatility spike is reaching for the wrong thing. Section 5.3 renames the mechanism and inherits that renaming's own limit: a first principal component of seven equity indices is a proxy for global systemic risk, not a measurement of it, so orthogonalising the block against it is weaker than orthogonalising against a factor built from a wider universe, and the remainder may still carry global content this basis cannot see. The direction of that test is informative; its magnitude is not a bound. Section 9.2 has a limit of the same shape: its severest rung holds 324 days, so a contribution confined to conditions more extreme than the ladder reaches would not appear, and nothing there rules one out.

The largest of these limits is the estimator, and we can be specific about it rather than gesturing, because we fitted the alternative. Every model in this paper is a ridge on a fixed feature set, chosen because it is transparent and because the object under study is the substitution rate, not the best attainable forecast. The natural competitor is a state-space model: one latent global volatility factor on an AR(1), every series loading on it, and a Kalman filter handling a missing observation by dropping that row from the measurement equation, which is what a delayed domestic mark is, not something to be imposed on the data. Fitted two-step after Doz, Giannone and Reichlin [21], with the state-space parameters estimated once on the initial training block and then frozen, and the filtered state fed into the same forecasting ridge on the same days, it beats the specification used throughout this paper at δ = 21 by 0.0124 of R², interval [+0.00114, +0.02192], and at δ = 55 by 0.0204, interval [+0.00728, +0.03109]. It loses at δ = 0, by 0.0233, interval [−0.03280, −0.01047], and the crossover is the interesting part: imposing a one-factor structure costs skill when the mark is fresh and pays when it is stale. Its reconstruction of the current domestic state that the forecaster is not allowed to see correlates 0.84 with the truth at eleven weeks, against 0.18 for the stale value itself.

We report this rather than adopting it, and the reason is a scope judgement a reader is entitled to disagree with. R(δ) is a ratio in which the same domestic control appears three times, so a better estimator raises numerator and denominator together and the rate is not simply the rate plus the improvement; recomputing every conditional and every robustness check inside a filtered specification is a different paper. What the result does establish is the direction and the size of what this paper's transparency costs: roughly two points of R² at the longest delay. That is the honest bound on the estimator limitation, and it is larger than several of the effects the paper argues over. The horizon evidence in Section 8.3 runs out at one month, where the rate has already fallen to 51% with an interval too wide to lean on; we do not know what happens at a quarter. Equity indices only. Close times are treated as standard, ignoring daylight saving, which moves them by an hour and never reorders the blocks. The index data are a retail export with no point-in-time guarantee, and one join in it is unverified: of the nine two-file exports, eight overlap and agree exactly on every overlapping row, while the S&P's two files abut without overlapping, so the only checks available there are that the join is contiguous and that no level shift appears across it. Section 3 gives both. That is the one series in the paper where a silent corruption at a single seam could not be detected by comparison, and it is the target series.

For VIX the same concern is now settled instead of flagged, because Cboe publishes its own daily history and we audited one against the other. On the 6,747 dates both carry, they are identical to the cent on 99.91% of rows and the mean absolute difference is 0.000222 index points; two rows fall outside this project's 0.5% seam tolerance, 28 November 2008 and 24 December 2012, both shortened sessions. The audit also found something we were not looking for. The retail export carries fifteen rows on dates United States equities were shut, 11 September 2001 among them: a fabricated observation rather than a discrepancy, and one no admissibility rule here could have caught, because the row is perfectly well formed. All fifteen fall outside the panel, so no model was ever shown one. VIX is Cboe's file throughout this paper; substituting it moved no cell of Section 8 by as much as a thousandth of R². We report that as measurement instead of reassurance, and note that a retail export of any series can contain rows the exchange never produced.

The official file does not, however, supply the observation Section 3 actually wants, because it carries one open, high, low and close per day and no intraday snapshot at all. The 21:00 value of VIX existed and is the right regressor; it is not in any daily product, official or retail, and the lagged and same-day arms bracket it without recovering it. Differences at δ ≤ 2 fall inside the simultaneous bands. The variance-scale loss is dominated by a handful of crisis days, so its intervals are wide enough to be uninformative at short delays.

13. Does a clean lag resemble a stale mark?

Section 14 will argue that what carries from this paper to an illiquid asset is the apparatus rather than the numbers. One part of that claim can be checked rather than hoped for, and this section checks it. Every result here models staleness as a clean lag: the forecaster sees the state exactly as it stood δ days ago and nothing since. A marked asset is not stale in that way. It is revised on a schedule and held flat in between, and the revision is a partial adjustment toward current evidence, not a full mark, which is how the property literature has modelled appraisal indices for thirty years [8]. If the substitution rate under that kind of staleness looked nothing like the rate under a clean lag, R(δ) would be a curve about clean lags alone.

It does not. Rebuilding the domestic block as a mark revised every q days and held between revisions, and matching on mean staleness, gives 64% against the clean lag's 60% at ten days, 71% against 70% at thirty-one, and 70% against 72% at ninety-four. Adding partial adjustment on top moves it a few points the other way, 63% where matching predicts 70%, so the equivalence is approximate and we claim no more than that. One thing we got wrong on the way is worth recording: our first attempt modelled appraisal as pure exponential smoothing, which is not staleness at all. A weighted average of the recent past still contains today's observation, so it costs almost nothing. R² falls from 0.499 to 0.456 across a mean lag of eleven weeks, against 0.499 to −0.035 under a clean lag, and leaves almost no damage for breadth to repair. Holding a number flat between revisions is what makes a mark stale, not blurring it.

14. What to do with this

Someone holding a stale mark on an asset with a liquid options market should buy the options data, and should buy the tenor that matches their forecast horizon, not the headline one. Section 8 provides strong evidence for the first, and the foreign cross-section adds nothing measurable once implied volatility is in hand. Section 8.5 provides it for the second: for a five-day question the nine-day index beats thirty-day VIX at every delay we test, by 0.024 to 0.041 of R², which is a larger prize than most of what the rest of this paper argues over and costs nothing but choosing the right series.

Someone holding a stale mark on an asset with no options market has breadth as the only candidate on the table, and it is no corner case: it is the case Section 1 opens with. Quarterly-marked private equity, appraised property and funds reporting a lagged net asset value have no listed option chain on the asset itself, so the substitute that wins Section 8 is not available to any of them. The redundancy result therefore narrows where the equity measurement transfers, not where the question matters. Sections 4 to 6 show how that worth can be measured and give a liquid-market benchmark for it, rather than an estimate for the illiquid asset itself: about 80% of the delay-induced loss recovered in stressed markets, where the estimate is stable and tight, 81% at eleven weeks with an interval of [74, 87], and an amount this sample cannot determine in calm ones. One practical point belongs with those numbers and is easy to miss, because the paper reports it as a robustness result. The rates above come from the prespecified seven-regressor model, which is not the model to run. Section 5.1 shows a single real-time factor beating all seven series at every delay and raising R(55) to 75.2%, and an equal-weighted mean of the seven does nearly as well for no estimation at all. The seven coefficients cost one to five points of R² to fit and buy nothing back. Anyone building this should compress the cross-section first and treat the seven-regressor figures as the conservative benchmark they are. The unconditional figure is 72% against the paper's own control and 68% against the stronger control of Section 10.1; we quote the conditional numbers because they are the ones that mean anything. Against that sits a cost of one to five points of R² for carrying the extra series. One condition comes with those figures, and Section 9.3 makes it explicit: the foreign data has to be current. Every rate quoted in this paper assumes the cross-section arrives the same evening, and a feed one day behind turns Table 14's 36% into 9% at three days and its 51% into 29% at five. Before buying breadth, check that it will arrive on time. A cross-section older than the mark it is replacing is worth nothing, and at the shortest delays it is worth less than nothing.

One generalisation is worth refusing explicitly. It would be convenient to present Figure 1 as a benchmark for how quickly information decays in delayed systems, and it is not one. Those curves are a measurement of one index against seven specific peers over one sample. The correlation between a private portfolio and the public cross-section is a different quantity, and nothing here bounds it. A reader who wants a number for their own asset will not find it in this paper.

What does carry across is the apparatus rather than the numbers. R(δ) is defined in units that survive a change of loss function, market or candidate. The delay sweep is a design anyone with a delayed series and a candidate substitute can run, and the horse race in Section 8 is the question to ask first, because its answer decides whether breadth is worth buying at all. The cost decomposition in Section 5 separates what a source knows from what a forecaster keeps, the distinction that turned our own most confusing result into an ordinary one. Pointed at an appraisal-based or quarterly-marked series, that machinery would produce the numbers this paper cannot, and the check above says the delay model it carries is the right shape for that series, even though the numbers are not.

15. What would test this next

The universe here is eight equity indices, and the obvious way to widen it is the one this paper predicts will fail. Section 5.3 splits the foreign block into its leading real-time component and the six components orthogonal to it, and finds the entire recovery in the first: the remainder is negative at every delay and beats the stale domestic model at none. A ninth and a tenth equity index would supply more of the same factor and more coefficients to estimate, and Section 5 has already priced that trade. Breadth within an asset class is not the frontier.

Breadth ACROSS asset classes is, and it is a sharper test than it first appears because this paper has to make a prediction about it. Sovereign bond futures, the major commodities and the G10 currencies all have observable daily ranges and therefore the same Yang-Zhang decision variable, so the design transfers with nothing changed but the panel. The stress ladder of Section 9.1 suggests they load on the same global risk factor, since cross-asset correlation is what converges in the episodes on that ladder. If they do, they will be redundant for the reason the orthogonal equity components are redundant, and the mechanism claimed here will have survived a considerably harder test than another equity index.

The way it breaks is the more interesting outcome, and it is specific enough to look for. Commodity volatility has drivers that no equity cross-section can see: an inventory report, a harvest, a production decision by a cartel. If a commodity block adds where equity breadth does not, then “a global volatility factor seen early” is too coarse a description of what is happening, and the right cross-section depends on what the stale asset IS, and is not one panel for everyone. A farmland book and a private credit book would then need different peers, which is a materially different recommendation from the one Section 14 gives and would be worth the trouble of discovering.

Foreign exchange should be run first, for a reason Section 9.3 makes plain, and for no reason about currencies. FX trades continuously across the week, so it has no admissible-timestamp problem at all: there is no previous session for a currency pair, no peer that closes after the target, and none of the shift rules in Section 3. That also means it is exempt from the penalty in Table 14, the largest practical threat this paper has to its own recommendation. A substitute that is current by construction is precisely what a holder of a stale mark wants, and of the candidates listed here FX is the only one that is current by construction, not by luck of the clock.

Two warnings for whoever runs it. The models are the easy part; the data hygiene is not. Futures require contract rolls, so a continuous series carries a seam every quarter instead of the single seam per export audited in Section 3, and the audit, not the estimation, is where that work will actually go. And the admissibility table stops being a list of closing times: a bond future and its underlying cash market can trade on different calendars, so the rule that a peer counts only when its timestamp precedes the target's needs restating instrument by instrument instead of country by country.

Three other threads are open and named where they arise: the illiquid-asset target this whole exercise is about and cannot reach, in Section 11; whether the rate keeps rising past one month, where Section 8.3's evidence runs out with an interval too wide to lean on; and, in the companion note [13], ranking forecasters against a tail target, which needs more independent volatility spikes than nine years of one index contains. The cross-asset panel proposed above would help with the last of those as a side effect, since spikes accumulate across markets as well as through time.

Appendix. A confidence set for a ratio whose denominator can vanish

The substitution rate is a ratio, and Section 7 reports that inverting a HAC t-test after Fieller leaves every published interval standing. This is how that was computed, and why it was not the first answer we got. Fieller's theorem inverts a t-test on the linear combination num − R·den, which is zero at the true rate, and solves the resulting quadratic for the set of rates not rejected. When the denominator is well separated from zero the solution is an ordinary interval close to the delta-method one. When it is not, the quadratic's leading coefficient changes sign and the confidence set is correctly a union of two half-lines, or the whole line: an honest statement that the data do not pin the ratio down, and one a percentile bootstrap cannot make. We have met the symptom in this project without naming it, in an interval of [−154%, +50%] at one point, and treated it as a nuisance when it is an identification problem. Two things about the calculation are worth recording. The variances must be HAC, with the same Bartlett kernel used in Section 7, because the five-day targets overlap: the first version used iid variances and returned intervals narrower than the block bootstrap at every delay, which is impossible for overlapping targets and was the only sign that anything was wrong. And δ = 0 is absent from the comparison throughout, because there the denominator is identically zero by construction, the one delay at which the ratio does not exist at all, rather than being merely hard to pin down.
